I Sold My Business - Now What Do I Do With The Money?

January 18, 2026 · 15 min read

You built something from nothing. You stressed, sacrificed, and probably didn't take a real vacation in years. And now someone's written you a check with more zeros than you've ever seen.

Congratulations. Seriously.

Now comes the hard part: not screwing it up.

I watched a friend sell his HVAC business for $2.3 million last year. Within 18 months, he'd made several decisions that cost him hundreds of thousands in unnecessary taxes and bad investments. Another friend sold her e-commerce company for slightly less but is now set for life because she made smarter moves.

The difference wasn't the sale price. It was what they did next.

Step Zero: Do Nothing (For Now)

I know this sounds counterintuitive when you're sitting on a pile of cash. But the worst financial decisions happen in the first 6 months after a windfall.

Park the money somewhere safe and boring:

  • High-yield savings account (currently 4-5%)
  • Treasury bills or money market funds
  • Short-term CDs

You're not trying to maximize returns right now. You're trying to not lose money while you figure out your plan. That 4% is fine. Making a rushed decision that costs you 20% is not fine.

Give yourself at least 3-6 months before making major moves. Use that time to assemble your team and build your strategy.

The Tax Bill Is Coming

This is the part that blindsides people. You sold for $2 million and you're imagining $2 million in your account. But Uncle Sam has other plans.

Capital gains tax

If you owned the business for over a year, you'll pay long-term capital gains rates on the profit: 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on your income, plus potentially 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax if you're above certain thresholds.

On a $2 million sale with $500,000 in basis (what you invested), you might owe $300,000-$400,000 in federal taxes alone. Plus state taxes if your state has them.

Ordinary income portion

Not everything gets capital gains treatment. If part of the sale is allocated to consulting agreements, non-compete clauses, or certain assets, that portion might be taxed as ordinary income – up to 37%.

Estimated taxes

If the sale closes in Q1, you might owe estimated taxes in Q2. If you don't pay them, penalties add up.

Set Aside Tax Money Immediately

Before you spend a dime, set aside 35-40% in a separate account earmarked for taxes. Overfund it. Getting a refund is better than getting a bill you can't pay.

Tax Strategies That Can Save Serious Money

Here's where good planning makes a massive difference.

Qualified Small Business Stock (QSBS) exclusion

If your business was a C-corp and you held the stock for over 5 years (plus other requirements), you might be able to exclude up to $10 million or 10x your basis from capital gains. This is huge if you qualify.

Installment sale

Instead of getting all cash at once, structuring the sale with payments over time can spread your tax liability across multiple years, keeping you in lower brackets.

Opportunity Zone investment

Rolling capital gains into a Qualified Opportunity Zone fund can defer and potentially reduce your tax bill. The rules are complex, but for the right situation, it's powerful.

Charitable strategies

Donating appreciated stock to a Donor-Advised Fund before the sale can reduce taxable gains while letting you take a charitable deduction. If you were planning to give anyway, the tax math here can be very favorable.

State residency planning

If your state has high income taxes and you were already thinking about moving to a no-income-tax state (like Florida, Texas, or Nevada), doing it BEFORE the sale can save significant money. But you need to establish genuine residency – not just claim it.

This needs to be done right. Moving to Florida the week before closing doesn't count. But if you've been planning a move to the Tampa Bay area anyway, accelerating that timeline could save you 5-13% in state taxes depending on where you're coming from.

Building Your Post-Sale Team

You can't navigate this alone. You need:

Tax attorney or CPA

Ideally one with experience in business sales. They should be involved BEFORE the sale closes to structure things tax-efficiently.

Investment manager or wealth advisor

This is a different skill set than what got you here. Running a business and managing a multi-million dollar portfolio are different disciplines.

What made them different was their focus on active tax management – which is exactly what you need when you have a large taxable event.

Their whole approach is built around minimizing the tax drag on investments. With mutual funds, you're at the mercy of when the fund realizes gains. With their actively managed portfolios, they can harvest losses, manage timing, and optimize for your specific tax situation. When you're dealing with business sale proceeds, that kind of tax awareness can easily be worth six figures over time.

Estate planning attorney

Your estate plan from when you had a small business is probably inadequate now. Time to update wills, trusts, powers of attorney.

Investment Strategy Post-Sale

Your investment approach needs to shift. When you owned the business, you were already heavily concentrated in one asset. Now you need diversification.

Don't put it all in one thing

No single stock. No single real estate deal. No crypto YOLO. You didn't take this risk when you had less money – don't start now.

Consider your human capital

If you're 45 and planning to start another business, your financial profile is different than if you're 62 and done working. Younger = can take more investment risk. Older = prioritize preservation.

Think about income needs

Does this money need to replace your business income? If so, you need a portfolio designed for withdrawals, not maximum growth.

Factor in taxes continuously

With this much money in taxable accounts, investment decisions have tax consequences. The difference between 15% and 37% on gains is massive. Every rebalance, every dividend, every sale needs tax consideration.

Common Post-Sale Mistakes

Buying a bunch of stuff immediately

New house, new car, boat, vacation property. Suddenly your pile of money is a pile of expenses. Lifestyle inflation after a windfall is the #1 wealth destroyer.

Investing in friends' businesses

"Hey, now that you have money, want to invest in my thing?" The answer should almost always be no. You got lucky once – don't assume you can pick winners as an investor.

Starting another business immediately

You're probably not thinking clearly yet. Give yourself at least a year before diving into another venture. And when you do, fund it from cash flow or a small portion of your assets – not the whole pile.

Trying to time the market

"The market seems high, I'll wait for a dip." Meanwhile, you miss 20% gains. Dollar-cost average in over 12-24 months if you're nervous, but don't sit in cash waiting for the perfect moment.

DIY investing with too much money

Managing a $50,000 portfolio yourself is fine. Managing $3 million yourself when you're emotionally attached to the money and don't have experience? Recipe for expensive mistakes.

The Identity Crisis Nobody Warns You About

This isn't just about money. For years, you WERE the business. Your identity, your purpose, your daily structure – all tied to it.

Now what?

A lot of business sellers go through a depression period 6-12 months after the sale. The money is great, but the meaning is gone. Plan for this:

  • Give yourself permission to do nothing for a while
  • But also plan for what comes next – a new project, board seats, consulting, philanthropy, whatever gives you purpose
  • Don't let financial decisions be driven by boredom (the "I'll just start day trading" trap)

A Reasonable First-Year Plan

Month 1-2: Park money safely. Assemble your team. Do nothing else.

Month 3-4: Tax planning with your CPA. Estate planning with attorney. Get investment strategy advice.

Month 5-6: Begin implementing investment strategy. Start dollar-cost averaging into your long-term allocation.

Month 7-12: Continue steady implementation. Avoid major lifestyle changes. Process the transition emotionally.

Year 2: Now you can start thinking about major purchases or new ventures – but from a position of clarity, not impulse.

The Bottom Line

Selling your business is a one-time event. What you do with the proceeds is a lifetime decision. Move slowly, get good advice, and remember: the goal isn't to look rich. The goal is to stay rich.
