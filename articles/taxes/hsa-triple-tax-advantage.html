The HSA: The Most Underrated Account in America

December 10, 2025 · 10 min read

If I could go back and tell my younger self one financial tip, it might be: "Max out your HSA." The Health Savings Account is genuinely the most powerful savings account available to most Americans, and most people either don't have one, don't use it right, or don't even know what it is.

Let me explain why this account is so special.

What Is an HSA?

A Health Savings Account is a tax-advantaged account that lets you save money for medical expenses. But here's the catch – you can only open one if you have a High Deductible Health Plan (HDHP).

For 2025, an HDHP must have:

  • Minimum deductible: $1,650 (individual) or $3,300 (family)
  • Maximum out-of-pocket: $8,300 (individual) or $16,600 (family)

If your health insurance qualifies, you can contribute to an HSA.

The Triple Tax Advantage

This is what makes the HSA special. No other account has all three of these benefits:

1. Tax-deductible contributions

The money you put in reduces your taxable income. If you contribute $4,000 and you're in the 22% bracket, you save $880 in taxes immediately.

2. Tax-free growth

Any investment gains in the account are never taxed. Compare this to a regular brokerage account where you pay capital gains tax, or even a 401(k) where you pay income tax on withdrawals.

3. Tax-free withdrawals

As long as you use the money for qualified medical expenses, you pay no tax on withdrawals. Ever.

Let me compare this to other accounts:

Account Contributions Growth Withdrawals
Traditional 401(k)/IRA Tax-free Tax-free Taxed
Roth 401(k)/IRA Taxed Tax-free Tax-free
Brokerage Taxed Taxed Taxed
HSA Tax-free Tax-free Tax-free*

*For qualified medical expenses

No other account goes tax-free → tax-free → tax-free. It's genuinely unique.

The Secret: You Don't Have to Spend It Now

Most people use their HSA like a checking account – put money in, spend it on medical stuff, empty it out. This completely misses the point.

Here's the power move: Invest your HSA and let it grow. Pay for current medical expenses out of pocket. Save receipts. Reimburse yourself years or decades later.

There's no time limit on reimbursements. You can pay $500 for a dental procedure today, save the receipt, let your HSA investments grow for 20 years, and then withdraw $500 tax-free whenever you want – while your investments have (hopefully) grown significantly.

The Numbers

Let's say you max out your HSA ($4,300/year individual, $8,550 family) from age 30 to 65.

Individual contribution over 35 years: $150,500

At 7% average return, that grows to approximately $600,000.

That's $600,000 that was never taxed going in, while growing, or coming out (for medical expenses). At a 22% tax rate, that's roughly $130,000+ in tax savings over a lifetime.

What Counts as "Qualified Medical Expenses"?

More than you think:

  • Doctor visits, hospital stays, surgery
  • Prescriptions and over-the-counter medications (since 2020)
  • Dental care (cleanings, fillings, braces, etc.)
  • Vision care (exams, glasses, contacts, LASIK)
  • Mental health services
  • Medical equipment (wheelchair, blood pressure monitor, etc.)
  • Health insurance premiums (in some situations)
  • Long-term care insurance
  • Medicare premiums (after 65)

The list is extensive. And here's the thing – healthcare costs in retirement are typically $300,000+ for a couple. That HSA money will absolutely get used.

After Age 65: Basically a Traditional IRA

If you reach 65 with HSA money leftover and no medical expenses to claim, you can withdraw for any reason and just pay income tax (like a traditional IRA). No 20% penalty like you'd face before 65.

So worst case, your HSA becomes a traditional IRA. Best case, it's completely tax-free for medical expenses. There's no bad outcome.

How to Maximize Your HSA

1. Contribute the maximum

For 2025: $4,300 individual, $8,550 family. If you're 55+, add $1,000 catch-up.

2. Invest it

Most HSA providers let you invest once you hit a certain threshold (often $1,000-2,000 in cash). Put the excess in low-cost index funds, just like your 401(k).

3. Pay medical expenses out of pocket if possible

Don't touch the HSA money. Let it grow. Pay for current expenses with regular money.

4. Save all your receipts

Every medical expense receipt. I use a folder in Google Drive. You can reimburse yourself years later.

5. Choose a good HSA provider

Your employer's HSA might have high fees or bad investment options. You can transfer to a better provider like Fidelity (no fees, good investment options) once per year.

The Catch: HDHP Requirement

To have an HSA, you need an HDHP. These plans have lower premiums but higher deductibles. You pay more out of pocket before insurance kicks in.

For healthy people with an emergency fund, HDHPs often make sense – you save on premiums and get HSA access. For people with chronic conditions or high ongoing medical costs, a traditional plan might be better despite losing HSA access.

Run the numbers for your situation. Compare:

  • HDHP premiums + expected out-of-pocket costs + max HSA contribution
  • vs. Traditional plan premiums + expected out-of-pocket costs

Factor in the tax savings from HSA contributions. Often the HDHP wins, especially if you're relatively healthy.

My HSA Strategy

Here's exactly what I do:

  1. Contribute the maximum through payroll (gets extra FICA tax savings)
  2. Keep $1,500 in cash for immediate medical needs
  3. Invest everything above that in a total stock market index fund
  4. Pay current medical expenses with my regular checking account
  5. Save all medical receipts in a folder
  6. Plan to let it grow until retirement, then reimburse myself for decades of saved receipts

Current HSA balance: about $35,000. Saved receipts: about $12,000 worth. I can withdraw $12,000 tax-free anytime I want, or let the full $35,000 keep growing.

The Priority Order

Given how powerful the HSA is, where does it fit in contribution priorities?

My suggested order:

  1. 401(k) up to employer match (free money)
  2. HSA maximum (triple tax advantage)
  3. 401(k) or IRA to maximum
  4. Taxable brokerage

Some people put HSA even before the 401(k) match due to its unique benefits. I wouldn't argue with that approach.

The Bottom Line

If you have access to an HSA and aren't maxing it out, you're probably leaving money on the table. The triple tax advantage is unmatched. Treat it like a retirement account that happens to have a medical expense bonus, not like a checking account for doctor visits.
