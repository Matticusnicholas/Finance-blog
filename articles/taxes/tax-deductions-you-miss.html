Tax Deductions You're Probably Missing

January 3, 2026 · 11 min read

I used to think tax deductions were only for rich people with accountants and complicated finances. Then I learned about a bunch of deductions and credits I'd been missing for years – regular stuff that applies to normal people with normal jobs.

Here's what I've learned about commonly missed tax breaks.

First: Deduction vs. Credit

Quick refresher:

  • Deduction: Reduces your taxable income. If you're in the 22% bracket, a $1,000 deduction saves you $220.
  • Credit: Reduces your actual tax bill dollar-for-dollar. A $1,000 credit saves you $1,000.

Credits are way more valuable. But deductions add up too.

Standard Deduction vs. Itemizing

For 2025, the standard deduction is about $14,600 (single) or $29,200 (married filing jointly). You only itemize if your itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction.

Since the 2017 tax law changes, most people take the standard deduction. But knowing what CAN be itemized helps you understand when crossing that threshold makes sense.

Deductions That Apply Even with Standard Deduction

These reduce your income BEFORE you take the standard deduction – they're called "above-the-line" deductions:

1. Traditional IRA Contributions

If you're eligible (income limits apply if you have a workplace retirement plan), you can deduct up to $7,000 ($8,000 if 50+) for traditional IRA contributions. This is one of the best deductions available.

Example: If you're in the 22% bracket and contribute $7,000, you save $1,540 in taxes.

2. HSA Contributions

Health Savings Account contributions are triple tax-advantaged: deductible going in, grow tax-free, and can be withdrawn tax-free for medical expenses. 2025 limits: $4,300 (individual) or $8,550 (family).

Even if you contribute through payroll, contributions above what you've already contributed can be deducted.

3. Student Loan Interest

You can deduct up to $2,500 in student loan interest paid, even if you take the standard deduction. Income limits apply (phases out at higher incomes).

If you're paying off student loans, make sure you're getting this deduction.

4. Educator Expenses

Teachers can deduct up to $300 for classroom supplies purchased out of pocket. This applies to K-12 teachers, instructors, counselors, principals, or aides who work at least 900 hours in a school year.

5. Self-Employment Tax Deduction

If you have self-employment income (side hustle, freelance, etc.), you can deduct half of your self-employment tax. This happens automatically if you file a Schedule SE.

Itemized Deductions Often Missed

If you itemize (or are close to the threshold), don't miss these:

1. State and Local Taxes (SALT)

You can deduct up to $10,000 in state and local taxes (property tax + income tax or sales tax). If you live in a high-tax state, you're probably hitting this cap.

2. Charitable Contributions

Cash donations to qualified charities are deductible if you itemize. But also remember:

  • Non-cash donations: Clothes, furniture, household items to Goodwill, etc. Keep receipts and document what you donated.
  • Mileage for charity work: If you drive for charitable purposes, you can deduct 14 cents per mile.
  • Out-of-pocket costs for volunteering: Supplies you buy for volunteer work can count.

3. Medical Expenses

Medical expenses exceeding 7.5% of your adjusted gross income are deductible. This is hard to hit, but if you had a big medical year (surgery, major illness, etc.), add it up. Include:

  • Health insurance premiums (if paid out of pocket, not through employer)
  • Prescriptions, doctor visits, hospital stays
  • Dental and vision care
  • Medical equipment
  • Mileage to medical appointments (67 cents/mile in 2025)

Tax Credits You Might Qualify For

Remember, credits are better than deductions because they reduce your tax bill directly:

1. Saver's Credit

If you earn under certain income thresholds ($38,250 single, $76,500 married in 2025) and contribute to a retirement account, you may qualify for a credit of 10-50% of contributions up to $2,000.

At 50%, that's a $1,000 credit for contributing $2,000 to your 401(k) or IRA. Free money on top of your retirement savings!

2. Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC)

This is a big one for lower-to-moderate income workers. It can be worth up to $7,830 for 2024 returns (depending on income and number of kids). Many people who qualify don't claim it.

Income limits vary but go up to about $59,000 for married filing jointly with 3+ kids.

3. Child Tax Credit

If you have kids, you probably know about this. But make sure you're getting the full amount you're entitled to. The credit is currently $2,000 per child under 17 (subject to income limits).

4. American Opportunity Tax Credit (Education)

Worth up to $2,500 per student for the first four years of college. This one's partially refundable, meaning you can get money back even if you don't owe taxes.

5. Lifetime Learning Credit

Worth up to $2,000 per tax return for education expenses. Unlike American Opportunity, this has no limit on years and covers graduate school and professional development courses.

6. Energy Credits

If you made energy-efficient improvements to your home, credits exist for:

  • Solar panels (30% of cost)
  • Heat pumps, water heaters, insulation (up to $3,200)
  • Electric vehicles (up to $7,500 for new, $4,000 for used)

Self-Employment and Side Hustle Deductions

If you have any self-employment income, you have deduction opportunities:

Home Office Deduction

If you use part of your home "regularly and exclusively" for business, you can deduct it. Simplified method: $5 per square foot up to 300 square feet = up to $1,500.

Business Expenses

Anything "ordinary and necessary" for your business: supplies, software, equipment, professional development, marketing, etc.

Vehicle Expenses

If you use your car for business, you can deduct 67 cents per mile (2025) or actual expenses. Keep a log!

Self-Employed Health Insurance

If you're self-employed and pay for your own health insurance, you can deduct 100% of premiums.

Easy Wins Most People Miss

  1. Max out HSA if you have one. Triple tax advantage is unbeatable.
  2. Contribute to traditional IRA if you can deduct it. Free tax savings.
  3. Track charitable donations, including non-cash. Those Goodwill runs add up.
  4. Check if you qualify for Saver's Credit. Easy money if you're under income limits.
  5. Track side hustle expenses. Even small businesses can reduce taxes significantly.

When to Get Professional Help

Consider a tax professional if:

  • You have significant self-employment income
  • You bought or sold property
  • You have complex investment situations
  • You're close to itemizing vs. standard deduction and want to optimize
  • You went through major life changes (marriage, divorce, kids, etc.)

The cost of a good tax preparer often pays for itself in found deductions.

My Approach

I track potential deductions year-round using a simple note on my phone. Every time I donate, buy something for work, or have a medical expense, I note it. At tax time, I have everything ready to go instead of trying to remember or dig through receipts.

The tax code is complicated, but you don't have to leave money on the table. Take the time to understand what you qualify for – it can add up to real savings.
