You Just Inherited Money: A Guide to Not Screwing It Up

January 25, 2026 · 12 min read

First, I'm sorry for your loss. Inheritances come with grief, and no amount of money makes that easier.

Second, the financial decisions you make in the next 6-12 months will determine whether this inheritance changes your life for the better or becomes a cautionary tale. Studies show that 70% of wealthy families lose their wealth by the second generation, and 90% by the third. Inheritances get squandered with alarming frequency.

Let's make sure you're in the minority who handle it well.

The First 30 Days: Do Almost Nothing

You're grieving. You're probably overwhelmed. This is not the time to make major financial decisions.

What to do:

  • Park any cash in a high-yield savings account or money market fund. Don't invest it yet.
  • Don't quit your job. Even if the inheritance is life-changing, give yourself time to process.
  • Don't tell many people. Money brings out weird behavior in others. Keep the circle small for now.
  • Don't make promises. Family members might ask for help. It's okay to say "I need time to figure things out."
  • Gather documents. Will, trust documents, account statements, property deeds. You'll need these later.

Seriously – the best thing you can do right now is nothing. The money will still be there in 90 days. Your clarity will be better.

Understanding What You've Actually Inherited

Inheritances come in different forms, each with different rules:

Cash

Simplest form. It's yours. No taxes due (inheritances aren't taxed as income in most cases). Park it and figure out next steps.

Investment accounts (taxable brokerage)

You get a "step-up in basis" – the cost basis resets to the value at the date of death. This is huge. If your parent bought stock for $10,000 that's now worth $100,000, you don't owe capital gains on that $90,000 gain. Your basis is $100,000.

This means you can sell immediately without tax consequences (other than gains/losses since the death date).

Retirement accounts (401k, IRA)

Rules changed in 2020. If you're not a spouse, you generally have to empty the account within 10 years. There are different rules for different beneficiary types – spouse, minor child, disabled beneficiary, etc.

These distributions will be taxable income. Plan accordingly.

Real estate

Also gets a step-up in basis. If the house was bought for $100,000 and is now worth $400,000, your basis is $400,000. You can sell without owing capital gains on the appreciation during your parent's lifetime.

But you'll need to deal with the property – maintain it, insure it, eventually sell or keep it.

Life insurance

Generally tax-free to beneficiaries. One of the cleanest forms of inheritance.

The Tax Landmines

While inheritances themselves generally aren't taxed as income, there are several tax issues to watch:

Inherited IRA distributions

These ARE taxable as ordinary income. A $500,000 inherited IRA doesn't mean $500,000 in your pocket – it might be $350,000 after taxes.

Strategy: Spread distributions over multiple years to avoid jumping into higher tax brackets.

Estate taxes

Only applies if the estate exceeds the federal exemption (~$13 million in 2024). Most people don't need to worry about this, but some states have lower thresholds.

Income in Respect of a Decedent (IRD)

Some assets (like unpaid salary, business income, or certain retirement account distributions) carry income tax liability to the heir. These don't get the step-up in basis.

Property taxes

In some states, inheriting real estate can trigger a property tax reassessment. California has specific rules around this. Know your state's laws.

Building Your Team

For a significant inheritance, you need professional help:

Estate attorney

To help navigate probate, understand the will/trust, and handle legal aspects of the transfer.

CPA or tax professional

To understand the tax implications and help with the decedent's final tax return (yes, someone has to file that).

Financial advisor or investment manager

For substantial inheritances, professional management is worth considering. You're dealing with sudden wealth – emotionally charged money that came from loss. Having a professional create distance between you and impulsive decisions is valuable.

The Psychology of Inherited Money

This is the part most people skip, and it's often what causes problems.

Inherited money feels different. It's:

  • Tied to grief. Spending it can feel like losing your loved one again.
  • Guilt-inducing. You might feel like you don't deserve it.
  • Pressure-filled. You don't want to "waste" their legacy.
  • Surreal. If you've never had money, suddenly having it can feel like it might disappear.

Common mistakes driven by these emotions:

  • Spending recklessly to avoid thinking about it
  • Never spending any of it (hoarding out of guilt)
  • Giving too much away to family members who pressure you
  • Making impulsive investments
  • Lifestyle inflation that burns through it quickly

Consider talking to a therapist, especially one who understands grief and financial transitions. This isn't weakness – it's wisdom.

A Framework for Deciding What to Do

After the initial 30-90 day waiting period, think through these questions:

1. What are my current financial gaps?

  • High-interest debt? Pay it off.
  • No emergency fund? Build one (6+ months of expenses).
  • Underfunded retirement? Max out tax-advantaged accounts.

2. What would make a meaningful difference in my life?

Maybe that's a house down payment, paying off student loans, or starting a business. One or two big moves, not twenty small ones.

3. What would honor the person who left this to me?

This is personal. Some people want to preserve and grow it. Others want to use it for experiences or education. There's no wrong answer, but thinking about it can provide clarity.

4. What's my plan for the rest?

After addressing immediate needs and one or two meaningful uses, the remainder should be invested for your future. This is where working with an investment professional helps.

Specific Situations

If you inherited a house

Decide: keep it, sell it, or rent it out? Each has implications:

  • Keep it: Emotional value, but you're responsible for taxes, maintenance, and insurance on a property you may not need.
  • Sell it: Cleanest option. You get the step-up in basis, so minimal/no capital gains. Invest the proceeds.
  • Rent it: Becomes an income property. But are you ready to be a landlord?

If you inherited an IRA

Understand the 10-year rule if it applies to you. Plan your distributions strategically to minimize taxes. You don't have to take equal amounts each year – you could take less in high-income years and more in low-income years.

If you inherited a business or business interest

Get professional help immediately. Valuation, tax implications, and operational questions all need expert guidance.

Protecting the Inheritance Long-Term

The goal isn't just to receive the inheritance – it's to preserve and grow it.

  • Keep it separate. Don't commingle inherited assets with joint accounts if you're married. In divorce, inherited assets are usually separate property – but only if you keep them separate.
  • Invest appropriately. Match your investment strategy to your timeline and goals. Don't be too conservative (inflation will erode it) or too aggressive (volatility might cause panic selling).
  • Update your own estate plan. You now have assets that need to go somewhere when you die. Do you have a will? Beneficiaries designated on accounts?
  • Be thoughtful about sharing. You're not obligated to give money to siblings, cousins, or anyone else. If you choose to help family, do it thoughtfully, not reactively.

The Ultimate Goal

Handle this inheritance well enough that you could pass on even more to the next generation – or to causes you care about. The gift you received can keep giving if you steward it wisely.

The best way to honor someone's legacy is to use their gift to build a better life – not just materially, but in security, freedom, and opportunity. Take your time, get good advice, and make decisions you'll be proud of in 20 years.
