Asset Allocation: The "Your Age in Bonds" Rule is Outdated

December 15, 2025 · 9 min read

You've probably heard the old rule: hold your age as a percentage in bonds. If you're 30, hold 30% bonds, 70% stocks. If you're 60, hold 60% bonds, 40% stocks. It's simple, easy to remember, and... increasingly wrong for most people.

Let me explain why this rule made sense decades ago, why it doesn't work as well today, and what I think about instead.

Why the Rule Existed

The logic was sound for its time:

  • Bonds are less volatile than stocks
  • As you get closer to retirement, you can't afford big losses
  • Therefore, gradually shift to bonds as you age

When this rule was created (mid-20th century), a few things were true that aren't true anymore:

  1. People died younger. Life expectancy was around 70. If you retired at 65, you needed your money to last maybe 10-15 years.
  2. Pensions were common. Most retirees had defined benefit pensions providing stable income. They didn't need their investment portfolio to last as long.
  3. Bond yields were higher. You could get 6-8% on bonds. They were a legitimate wealth-building tool, not just a volatility reducer.

Why It Doesn't Work Today

We Live Longer

A 65-year-old today has a good chance of living to 85 or beyond. That's 20+ years of retirement. You need your money to grow for decades, not just sit there. If you're 60% bonds at retirement, you're basically betting on zero growth for the rest of your life.

Worse, you might outlive your money. A portfolio that's too conservative won't keep up with inflation over a 20-30 year retirement.

Pensions Are Gone

Most people retiring today don't have pensions. They have 401(k)s and IRAs that they have to manage themselves. These portfolios need to both generate retirement income AND continue growing. That requires more stocks than the old rule suggests.

Bond Yields Have Been Historically Low

For most of the last 15 years, bond yields have been terrible. We're talking 1-3% returns – barely keeping up with inflation. Even with recent rate increases, bonds aren't the wealth builders they used to be.

A 40-year-old holding 40% bonds in a low-yield environment is basically saying "I'm okay with 40% of my money growing slower than inflation."

What I Think About Instead

Rather than a simple age-based formula, I think about asset allocation based on these factors:

1. Time Horizon

When do you actually need this money? Not just "retirement" but specifically which portions of your retirement?

  • Money you need in 0-5 years: Keep it safe (bonds, cash, stable value funds)
  • Money you need in 5-10 years: Mix of stocks and bonds
  • Money you don't need for 10+ years: Can be mostly stocks, even in retirement

If you're 60 and retiring at 65, not all your money is needed at 65. Some of it won't be touched until you're 75 or 80. That portion can still be in stocks.

2. Other Income Sources

Do you have Social Security? A pension? Rental income? The more stable income you have from other sources, the more aggressive your portfolio can be, because you don't depend on it as much for day-to-day expenses.

If Social Security will cover your basic expenses, your portfolio is just for extras and can take more risk.

3. Risk Tolerance (Honest Assessment)

Here's the uncomfortable question: When the market dropped 34% in March 2020, what did you do? If you panic-sold, a more conservative allocation might be right for you regardless of what the math says. The best allocation is one you can stick with during the bad times.

I've seen people with "aggressive" portfolios sell everything at the bottom because they couldn't handle the volatility. Their optimal allocation wasn't optimal at all because they couldn't execute it.

4. Total Financial Picture

Your investments aren't your whole financial life. Consider:

  • Your house (real estate is a kind of investment)
  • Your job stability and earning potential
  • Other assets like rental properties or business equity
  • Emergency fund and cash reserves

If you have a stable government job and a paid-off house, your "bonds" are effectively built in. You can afford more stocks in your portfolio.

Modern Allocation Frameworks

Age Minus 10 or 20

Some advisors now suggest holding "your age minus 10" or "your age minus 20" in bonds. So a 30-year-old might hold 10-20% bonds instead of 30%. This acknowledges longer lifespans and lower bond yields.

Target Date Funds

These automatically adjust allocation over time. The specific "glide path" varies by provider. Vanguard's 2055 fund, for example, starts at 90% stocks and gradually shifts more conservative. It handles the rebalancing for you.

I think these are great for most people. Not perfect, but good enough and completely automatic.

Bucket Strategy

Divide your portfolio into "buckets" based on when you need the money:

  • Bucket 1 (1-2 years of expenses): Cash and short-term bonds
  • Bucket 2 (3-7 years of expenses): Intermediate bonds and conservative stocks
  • Bucket 3 (everything else): Stocks for long-term growth

This way, even in retirement, you have a significant portion in stocks because you don't need that money for 7+ years.

My Current Allocation (For Reference)

I'm in my mid-30s. Here's what I do:

  • Retirement accounts: 90% stocks, 10% bonds
  • Taxable brokerage: 85% stocks, 15% bonds
  • Emergency fund: High-yield savings (not invested)

This is more aggressive than "age in bonds" would suggest (35% bonds). I'm comfortable with this because:

  1. I don't need this money for 30 years
  2. I have a stable job and solid emergency fund
  3. I've proven through 2020 and 2022 that I don't panic-sell
  4. I have decades for any crashes to recover

Will I adjust this as I get older? Yes, gradually. But I'll probably still be 70% stocks at age 60, not 40%.

The Most Important Thing

More important than your exact allocation is whether you can stick to it. The perfect allocation that you abandon during a downturn is worse than a mediocre allocation you stick with.

If you're using a target-date fund and it's "good enough," that might be better than a "perfect" allocation you constantly second-guess.

And remember: you can always adjust. If you realize you're more risk-averse than you thought (or more risk-tolerant), adjust accordingly. This isn't a one-time decision set in stone.

Quick Decision Framework

If you're more than 10 years from retirement: Lean heavily toward stocks (80%+ unless you know you can't handle volatility)

If you're 5-10 years from retirement: Start thinking about reducing risk, maybe 70-80% stocks

If you're in retirement: Keep more in stocks than traditional advice suggests (maybe 50-60%), but ensure you have 2-3 years of expenses in safe assets

The old rules were fine for their era. But we live longer, work differently, and face a different investment environment. Don't follow a formula that was designed for a world that no longer exists.
