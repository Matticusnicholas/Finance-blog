Credit Score Myths That Are Costing You Money

January 5, 2026 · 9 min read

Credit scores are surrounded by more myths and misinformation than almost any other financial topic. I've heard people confidently give advice that's completely wrong – and sometimes harmful to their credit.

Let me bust the most common myths and explain what actually matters.

Myth #1: "Carrying a Balance Helps Your Credit Score"

The myth: You need to carry a balance on your credit card and pay interest to build credit.

The truth: This is 100% false and I have no idea where it came from, but it refuses to die. You do NOT need to pay interest to build credit. Ever.

What actually happens: Your credit score cares about whether you use your credit and pay on time. It does NOT care whether you pay in full or carry a balance. Pay in full every month. Carrying a balance just costs you interest money for no benefit.

The confusion might come from "credit utilization" – yes, having some activity on your cards is better than having zero usage. But that activity can be a single purchase paid off immediately. You don't need to carry debt.

What to Do

Use your credit cards for normal purchases. Pay the full statement balance by the due date. Never pay credit card interest thinking it helps your score.

Myth #2: "Checking Your Credit Hurts Your Score"

The myth: Looking at your own credit report damages your credit score.

The truth: There are two types of credit inquiries:

  • Soft inquiries (checking your own credit, pre-approval checks, background checks) – These do NOT affect your score at all.
  • Hard inquiries (applying for credit cards, loans, mortgages) – These can slightly affect your score, typically 5-10 points, and the impact fades within a year.

Checking your own credit is a soft inquiry. You can check it every day if you want – it won't hurt anything. In fact, you SHOULD check it regularly to catch errors and fraud.

Free places to check: AnnualCreditReport.com (official free annual reports from all three bureaus), Credit Karma, your bank's app (many now include free scores).

Myth #3: "You Should Avoid Credit Cards Entirely"

The myth: Credit cards are dangerous and you shouldn't have any.

The truth: For some people with spending problems, this might be good advice. But for most people, having and responsibly using credit cards is actually important for building credit history.

No credit history can be as problematic as bad credit history. When you need a mortgage, auto loan, or apartment, having no credit history makes things difficult.

Credit cards, used responsibly, are tools that:

  • Build your credit history
  • Provide purchase protection
  • Offer fraud protection better than debit cards
  • Give you cash back or travel rewards

The key is "used responsibly" – that means paying in full, not spending more than you would with cash, and not opening tons of new accounts.

Myth #4: "Closing Old Cards Helps Your Credit"

The myth: You should close credit cards you don't use anymore.

The truth: Closing old cards often HURTS your credit, for two reasons:

  1. Credit utilization increases. If you have $10,000 in total credit limits and $2,000 in balances, your utilization is 20%. If you close a card with a $5,000 limit, now your utilization is 40% ($2,000 / $5,000), which is worse.
  2. Average age of accounts decreases. Length of credit history matters. Closing your oldest cards reduces your average account age.

The exception: If a card has an annual fee and you don't use the benefits, closing it might make sense. Just understand the potential credit impact.

For no-fee cards you don't use: keep them open, maybe put a small recurring charge on them (like a streaming subscription) to keep them active, and let them age.

Myth #5: "All Debt Is Bad for Your Credit"

The myth: Having any debt hurts your credit score.

The truth: Having some debt that you pay on time actually helps your credit. The scoring models want to see that you can manage credit responsibly.

What matters is:

  • Payment history (paying on time)
  • Credit utilization (not using too much of your available credit)
  • Mix of credit types (having different kinds of accounts)
  • Length of history (longer is better)

A mortgage, auto loan, or credit card with a balance you're paying responsibly is NOT hurting your credit. Being maxed out on credit cards or missing payments – that hurts your credit.

Myth #6: "You Only Have One Credit Score"

The myth: There's one single credit score that everyone uses.

The truth: You have dozens of different credit scores. There are multiple versions of FICO scores, plus VantageScore, plus industry-specific scores for auto loans and mortgages.

The three major credit bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) each have their own data, which can differ. And each can calculate multiple scores using that data.

This is why the free score you see on Credit Karma might be different from what a mortgage lender pulls. They're literally different scores.

Don't obsess over the specific number. Focus on the factors that improve ALL your scores: pay on time, keep utilization low, maintain long credit history.

Myth #7: "Paying Off Collections Removes Them From Your Report"

The myth: If you pay a collection account, it disappears from your credit report.

The truth: Paying a collection doesn't automatically remove it. The account stays on your report for 7 years from the original delinquency date, whether you pay it or not.

That said, newer FICO scoring models (FICO 9 and newer) ignore paid collections. And some collectors will agree to "pay for delete" where they remove the account if you pay. But this isn't guaranteed.

Also: Paid collections look better to human loan officers, even if the scoring impact is debatable. When you're applying for a mortgage, underwriters review your report manually, not just the score.

What Actually Matters for Your Credit Score

The factors that influence your FICO score, in rough order of importance:

Factor Weight What It Means
Payment History 35% Do you pay on time? This is the biggest one.
Credit Utilization 30% How much of your available credit are you using?
Length of History 15% How long have you had credit accounts?
Credit Mix 10% Do you have different types of accounts?
New Credit 10% Have you opened many new accounts recently?

Simple Rules for Good Credit

Forget all the myths. If you do these things, you'll have good credit:

  1. Pay every bill on time, every time. Set up autopay if you need to. This is the most important factor.
  2. Keep credit card balances low. Try to stay under 30% utilization, ideally under 10%. Pay in full each month if possible.
  3. Don't open too many new accounts at once. Space out applications by several months.
  4. Keep old accounts open. Even if you don't use them. Age matters.
  5. Check your credit reports annually. Dispute any errors you find.

That's really it. Credit scoring is complicated under the hood, but the recipe for good credit is simple. Pay on time, don't overuse your credit, and let your accounts age.

Quick Credit Score Ranges

  • 800+: Exceptional – You'll get the best rates on everything
  • 740-799: Very Good – Still excellent rates, minimal difference from 800+
  • 670-739: Good – Acceptable to most lenders, decent rates
  • 580-669: Fair – Some lenders will work with you, but higher rates
  • Below 580: Poor – Difficulty getting approved, need to rebuild
