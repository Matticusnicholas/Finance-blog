Debt Snowball vs Avalanche: Which Actually Works Better?

December 22, 2025 · 10 min read

When I had $32,000 in various debts, I needed a system. Two methods kept coming up: the debt snowball (pay smallest balance first) and debt avalanche (pay highest interest first). I tried both during my payoff journey.

Here's my real experience with each method, and my thoughts on which one works better.

The Two Methods Explained

Debt Snowball (Dave Ramsey Style)

  1. List all debts from smallest balance to largest
  2. Pay minimums on everything except the smallest
  3. Throw every extra dollar at the smallest debt
  4. When it's paid off, take that payment and add it to the next smallest
  5. Repeat until debt-free

Why it works: Quick wins build momentum and motivation. Seeing debts disappear feels good and keeps you going.

Debt Avalanche (Math-Optimal)

  1. List all debts from highest interest rate to lowest
  2. Pay minimums on everything except the highest rate
  3. Throw every extra dollar at the highest interest debt
  4. When it's paid off, move to the next highest rate
  5. Repeat until debt-free

Why it works: Mathematically optimal. You pay the least total interest and become debt-free slightly faster (in most cases).

My Debt Situation

Here's what I was dealing with:

Debt Balance Interest Rate Min Payment
Credit Card A $4,200 22.99% $84
Credit Card B $1,800 19.99% $36
Personal Loan $8,000 11.5% $180
Student Loan $18,000 5.5% $195

Year 1: The Avalanche Attempt

I started with the debt avalanche because it's mathematically optimal. My highest interest debt was the $4,200 credit card at 22.99%.

Every month, I paid minimums on everything else and threw $400 extra at that card. The math was satisfying – I was saving money on interest.

But after 6 months, I had only paid off about $2,400 of that card. I still had over $30,000 in total debt. The progress felt invisible. Every time I checked my debt totals, the number was still depressing.

I got discouraged. Some months, I'd slack off and just pay minimums everywhere. "What's the point?" I'd think. "I'm going to be in debt forever anyway."

This is where the math starts to fail. The avalanche is optimal IF you execute it perfectly. But I wasn't executing it perfectly because I was demoralized.

Year 2: Switching to Snowball

I switched strategies. Instead of attacking the 22.99% card, I redirected everything to the $1,800 card (which was actually now closer to $1,500 after minimum payments).

Within 4 months, it was gone. Completely paid off. Zero balance.

That felt AMAZING. One debt, done. Crossed off the list. The dopamine hit was real.

Now I had that $36 minimum payment plus my $400 extra going toward the original big credit card. Combined with its minimum payment, I was putting $520/month toward it. It was gone in about 8 more months.

Then the personal loan. Then (eventually) the student loan.

The Numbers: What I Actually Paid

If I had perfectly executed the avalanche from the start, I would have paid about $34,800 total (principal plus interest).

With the snowball (including my failed avalanche attempt), I paid about $35,400 total.

That's $600 more in interest over the 3-year payoff journey. Or about $17/month.

But here's the thing: during the failed avalanche period, I was paying extra inconsistently because I was discouraged. If I account for those months of low motivation, the snowball method might have actually been FASTER in real life, even if it was slower in theory.

Why Psychology Beats Math

The debt avalanche assumes you're a robot who will execute perfectly regardless of emotions. The debt snowball assumes you're a human who needs motivation to keep going.

For most people, the psychological benefits of the snowball outweigh the mathematical benefits of the avalanche:

  • Quick wins build momentum. Paying off that first small debt feels like progress. It proves the system works.
  • Fewer accounts to manage. Each debt you eliminate is one less payment to track, one less statement to open.
  • Growing snowball is motivating. Watching your payment amount grow ($36 becomes $36 + $84 = $120, etc.) is exciting.
  • You actually stick with it. The best plan is the one you execute. A suboptimal plan executed well beats an optimal plan executed poorly.

When Avalanche Makes Sense

That said, there are situations where the avalanche is the better choice:

The interest rate difference is huge

If you have a $2,000 store card at 29.99% and a $1,500 card at 12%, the math savings become significant. Might be worth tackling the high-rate card first.

The balances are similar

If all your debts are roughly the same size, just go by interest rate. There's no psychological advantage to snowball if there's no "quick win" available.

You're genuinely motivated by math

Some people find "I'm saving the most money possible" more motivating than "I'm eliminating the most accounts." If that's you, avalanche might work better.

You have strong self-discipline

If you can stick to a plan regardless of emotional state, avalanche will save you money. But be honest with yourself about this.

The Hybrid Approach

What I actually recommend is a hybrid:

  1. Attack any tiny debts first. If you have a $500 medical bill, pay it off immediately. The quick win gets you started.
  2. Then look at interest rates. Among your remaining debts, are any significantly higher than others? Prioritize those.
  3. If rates are similar, go by balance. Take the wins where you can get them.

This gets you the best of both worlds: some quick wins for motivation, but still prioritizing high-interest debt when it matters most.

What About Balance Transfers?

A 0% balance transfer card can change the calculation entirely. If you can move high-interest debt to a 0% card, now the interest rate comparison doesn't matter (at least until the promo period ends).

I did this partway through my journey – transferred $3,000 from the 22.99% card to a new card with 0% for 18 months. This let me use the snowball method without worrying about the high-rate card accruing interest.

Just be careful: balance transfer fees (usually 3-5%) reduce the benefit, and if you don't pay it off before the promo ends, you're back to high interest.

The Bottom Line

If you're a regular person with regular emotions, the debt snowball will probably work better for you. The motivation from quick wins is real, and the "lost" interest is usually minor.

If you're highly disciplined or the interest rate differential is massive, the avalanche might be worth the psychological grind.

But honestly? Pick either one and stick with it. The difference between them is small compared to the difference between having a strategy and not having one.

The real enemy isn't using the "wrong" method. It's not having a plan at all.

Quick Decision Guide

Use Snowball if: You need motivation, have many small debts, or tend to give up on things

Use Avalanche if: You're math-motivated, your highest rate debt is relatively small, or rates differ significantly

Use Hybrid if: You want some quick wins but also have one debt with a significantly higher rate
