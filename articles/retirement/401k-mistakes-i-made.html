5 Costly 401(k) Mistakes I Made (So You Don't Have To)

January 10, 2026 · 10 min read

When I got my first real job with a 401(k), I was clueless. I vaguely knew it was "retirement savings" and that I should "probably do something with it," but that was about it. What followed was a decade of missed opportunities that probably cost me $50,000+ in retirement savings.

Let me share my mistakes so you can avoid them.

Mistake #1: Not Starting Immediately

When HR handed me the 401(k) enrollment paperwork during onboarding, I set it aside. "I'll figure this out later," I thought. I was 23, broke, and retirement seemed impossibly far away. The paperwork sat on my desk for six months before I finally filled it out.

During those six months, my company was matching contributions up to 4% of my salary. I earned $45,000. That's $1,800 in free money I didn't collect. Plus whatever growth that $1,800 (plus my own contributions) would have generated over the next 40+ years.

With compound interest, that six-month delay probably cost me $15,000+ by retirement.

The Lesson

If your employer offers a 401(k), enroll immediately. Even if you can only contribute 1%, do it on day one. You can figure out the details later – the important thing is to be enrolled and capturing any employer match.

Mistake #2: Not Maximizing the Employer Match

When I finally enrolled, I contributed 3% of my salary. My employer matched 100% up to 4%. This means if I contributed 4%, they'd add another 4% – literally doubling my contribution up to that point.

By only contributing 3%, I was leaving 1% of my salary on the table. That's $450/year in free money I declined. Over the 4 years I stayed at that job, I left $1,800+ in matching funds alone.

An employer match is the closest thing to free money you'll ever get in investing. It's an immediate 100% return. There's no investment in the world that guarantees that.

The Lesson

At minimum, contribute enough to get the full employer match. If they match up to 4%, contribute 4%. If they match up to 6%, contribute 6%. Figure out the rest of your budget around this. The match is non-negotiable.

Mistake #3: Leaving Money in the Default "Stable Value" Fund

This one hurts. For THREE YEARS, my 401(k) contributions sat in the default stable value fund because I never logged in to change the investment allocation.

Stable value funds are essentially like a savings account – very safe, but very low returns. During those three years, my money earned maybe 2% per year. The stock market returned about 12% per year during the same period.

Let's say I had $10,000 in there after year one. At 2% growth for three years, I'd have about $10,612. At 12% growth, I'd have about $14,049. That's almost $3,500 in missed growth – just in the first three years.

And that gap compounds over time. At retirement, that mistake probably cost me $20,000+.

The Lesson

Log in to your 401(k) and CHECK YOUR INVESTMENTS. If you're young (decades from retirement), you should probably be in mostly stock funds, not stable value or money market funds. Target-date funds are a great set-it-and-forget-it option.

Mistake #4: Cashing Out When Changing Jobs

When I left my first job, I had about $8,000 in my 401(k). I had no idea what to do with it, and someone told me I could just "cash it out." So I did.

What I didn't understand:

  • I'd pay income tax on the full amount (let's say 22% = $1,760)
  • I'd pay a 10% early withdrawal penalty ($800)
  • I'd lose all future growth on that money forever

After taxes and penalties, I got about $5,440. I probably used it to buy furniture or something forgettable.

If I had rolled that $8,000 into an IRA instead and let it grow at 7% for 35 years, it would have been worth about $85,000 at retirement. Instead, I bought a couch.

The Lesson

NEVER cash out your 401(k) when changing jobs. Either leave it where it is (if the plan allows), roll it into your new employer's plan, or roll it into an IRA. The tax penalties for early withdrawal are brutal, and you lose decades of compound growth.

Mistake #5: Picking High-Fee Funds

My second employer's 401(k) had terrible fund options. Well, mostly terrible. There was one low-cost index fund with a 0.05% expense ratio. There was also an actively managed "growth fund" with a 1.2% expense ratio that had done well recently.

Guess which one I picked? The expensive one, because it had better recent returns. I figured the higher fee was worth it for "better management."

Over the 5 years I was at that job, the expensive fund actually underperformed the cheap index fund (as most actively managed funds do). AND I paid 20x more in fees.

On a $50,000 balance:

  • 0.05% fee = $25/year
  • 1.2% fee = $600/year

That's $575/year in unnecessary fees. Over 5 years, that's almost $3,000 in fees alone, not counting the opportunity cost of lower returns.

The Lesson

Look for the lowest-cost index funds in your 401(k). Expense ratios matter. A 1% difference in fees can cost you 20-25% of your total retirement savings over 30 years. Always check the expense ratio before selecting a fund.

What I Do Now

After all these expensive lessons, here's my current 401(k) strategy:

Contribute at least enough for the full match

This is rule number one. If my employer matches 5%, I contribute at least 5%. I'm actually contributing more than that now, but the match is the floor.

Max it out if possible

The 2025 401(k) contribution limit is $23,500 (plus $7,500 catch-up if you're 50+). I try to hit this limit, though I wasn't always able to when I was younger. Every dollar you contribute grows tax-deferred.

Pick low-cost index funds

I look for S&P 500 index funds or total market index funds with expense ratios under 0.1%. If your plan doesn't have good options, complain to HR. Some companies will add better funds if employees ask.

Set it and forget it (mostly)

I rebalance once a year to make sure my allocation still matches my goals. Otherwise, I don't touch it. I definitely don't panic-sell during market downturns.

Roll over when changing jobs

When I leave an employer, I roll my 401(k) into an IRA at Fidelity. Never cash out. Never leave it behind in a forgotten account.

Quick 401(k) Checklist

If you're not sure about your 401(k) situation, run through this list:

  1. Are you enrolled? If not, do it TODAY.
  2. Are you contributing enough to get the full employer match? If not, increase your contribution.
  3. Do you know what your money is invested in? Log in and check.
  4. Are the expense ratios on your funds under 0.5%? If not, look for cheaper options.
  5. Is your allocation appropriate for your age? (Young = more stocks, older = more bonds)
  6. Do you have any old 401(k)s from previous jobs? Consider rolling them into an IRA.

If you can answer "yes" to all of these, you're in good shape. If not, you have homework to do. Don't make my mistakes.

One More Thing

If you have both a Traditional and Roth 401(k) option, consider the Roth if you're young and in a lower tax bracket. You pay taxes now but withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. I wish I'd known about Roth options earlier.
