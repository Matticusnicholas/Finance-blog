The Early Retirement Transition: What Nobody Tells You About the First Year

January 30, 2026 · 15 min read

You've spent years – maybe decades – accumulating. Saving, investing, watching the numbers grow. And now you're crossing over to the other side: decumulation. Spending down what you've built.

This transition is harder than most people expect, and not just financially. The psychological shift from "save everything" to "it's okay to spend" is genuinely difficult. And there are practical financial landmines that can trip you up.

I've talked to a lot of early retirees over the past few years, including several in my network here in St. Pete. Here's what they wish they'd known going in.

The Healthcare Cliff

If you retire before 65, healthcare is your biggest financial challenge. You're too young for Medicare, and you've lost employer coverage.

Your options:

COBRA: Continue your employer coverage for up to 18 months, but you pay the full premium (employer no longer subsidizes). This is often $1,500-$2,500/month for family coverage. Expensive, but it bridges a gap if you're close to 65.

ACA Marketplace: Healthcare.gov plans. Here's where strategy matters: your premium subsidy depends on your income. Early retirees can often control their income through careful withdrawal planning, keeping it low enough to qualify for substantial subsidies.

Health sharing ministries: Not insurance, but an alternative some people use. Understand the limitations before choosing this route.

Part-time work with benefits: Some people work part-time at companies that offer health benefits to part-timers (Starbucks is famously one). Not true "retirement," but a bridge strategy.

The ACA Strategy

Keep your MAGI (Modified Adjusted Gross Income) between 100% and 400% of the federal poverty level to maximize ACA subsidies. For a couple in 2024, that's roughly $20,000-$80,000. This might mean managing your investment withdrawals, Roth conversions, and realized capital gains carefully.

The Income Puzzle: Where Does the Money Come From?

You've got assets in various buckets. The order and method you withdraw from them matters for taxes and longevity.

Typical buckets:

  • Taxable brokerage: Most flexible. Dividends and capital gains taxed, but you can control timing.
  • Traditional 401(k)/IRA: Tax-deferred. Every dollar withdrawn is taxed as ordinary income.
  • Roth 401(k)/IRA: Tax-free withdrawals (after 5 years and 59½). The gold standard for tax-free income.
  • Cash/savings: Safe but earning less than inflation long-term.
  • Other: Rental income, pensions, annuities, etc.

The conventional wisdom:

Spend taxable first, then tax-deferred, then Roth last. This lets tax-advantaged accounts grow longer.

The smarter approach:

Consider filling up low tax brackets with tax-deferred withdrawals even when you don't need the money. Convert it to Roth. This is called Roth conversion laddering, and it can save significant taxes over a 30+ year retirement.

Example: If you're early retired and have minimal income, you might be in the 0%, 10%, or 12% bracket. Withdrawing traditional IRA money now (or converting to Roth) at these low rates is better than being forced to take RMDs later at potentially higher rates.

The Sequence of Returns Risk

This is the early retiree's nightmare: a market crash in your first few years of retirement.

When you're accumulating, market drops are actually good – you buy more shares at lower prices. But when you're withdrawing, market drops are devastating. You're selling shares at low prices, permanently reducing your portfolio's ability to recover.

Example: Two people retire with $1 million, withdrawing $40,000/year.

  • Person A gets 15% returns in year 1, then -10% in year 5
  • Person B gets -10% in year 1, then 15% in year 5

Same average return. But Person B's portfolio is significantly smaller by year 10. The early loss, combined with withdrawals, created a hole that's hard to dig out of.

Mitigation strategies:

  • Cash buffer: Keep 2-3 years of expenses in cash/short-term bonds. Withdraw from this during downturns instead of selling stocks low.
  • Flexible spending: Be prepared to cut discretionary spending during market downturns.
  • Part-time income: Even small amounts earned in the early years dramatically improve portfolio survival rates.
  • Conservative first-year withdrawal rate: Maybe start at 3.5% instead of 4% to build a buffer.

The 4% Rule: Still Valid?

The "4% rule" says you can withdraw 4% of your initial portfolio annually (adjusted for inflation) with a high probability of not running out over 30 years. It's based on historical data.

For early retirees, it has limitations:

  • Your retirement might be 40-50 years, not 30
  • It was based on a 50/50 or 60/40 portfolio – your allocation might differ
  • Future returns might be lower than historical averages
  • It doesn't account for healthcare costs or other early-retirement-specific expenses

Many financial planners now suggest 3.5% or even 3% for very early retirees. This requires a larger nest egg but provides more margin for error.

Social Security Strategy

Even early retirees will eventually get Social Security. The question is when to claim.

You can claim at:

  • 62: Reduced benefit (~70% of full)
  • 67 (for most current workers): Full benefit
  • 70: Maximum benefit (~124% of full)

For early retirees, delaying often makes sense because:

  1. You'll likely have a long retirement, so more years to collect the higher benefit
  2. It's a longevity hedge – if you live into your 90s, the delayed benefit pays off enormously
  3. Your early retirement years can be funded from investments, preserving the option to delay

But it's not always clear-cut. Health, spousal considerations, and other income sources all factor in.

The Psychological Transition

This is the part nobody prepares you for.

You've spent your career defining yourself by your work, your title, your productivity. Now what?

Common struggles in the first year:

  • Loss of identity: "What do I do?" becomes "Who am I?"
  • Guilt about spending: After years of saving, spending feels wrong
  • Decision fatigue: Every day is unstructured. What do I do with all this time?
  • Social isolation: Work friends disappear. Your spouse is still working. Who do you hang out with?
  • Purpose vacuum: Without work problems to solve, what's your reason to get up?

Research shows that the happiest retirees have:

  • Social connections outside of work
  • Meaningful activities (volunteering, hobbies, part-time work)
  • Some structure to their days
  • Goals and projects to work toward

The financial part of retirement is actually the easy part. The identity part is harder.

The First Year Checklist

Before you retire:

  • ☐ Healthcare plan in place
  • ☐ 2-3 years of cash reserves beyond emergency fund
  • ☐ Withdrawal strategy planned (which accounts, what order)
  • ☐ Tax professional consulted on Roth conversion strategy
  • ☐ Estate documents updated
  • ☐ Social calendar/activities planned (don't underestimate this)

Month 1-3:

  • ☐ Set up systematic withdrawals or distributions
  • ☐ Establish new healthcare coverage
  • ☐ Create a realistic budget based on actual spending
  • ☐ Find your rhythm – what does a good week look like?

Month 3-6:

  • ☐ Review spending vs. plan – adjust if needed
  • ☐ Begin Roth conversion strategy if applicable
  • ☐ Evaluate if you actually enjoy this (it's okay if you don't)

Month 6-12:

  • ☐ First tax filing in retirement – learn how your new situation works
  • ☐ Rebalance portfolio if needed
  • ☐ Assess whether you need to work part-time (financially or psychologically)

The Permission to Spend

One of the strangest challenges: allowing yourself to spend money you've saved.

I've talked to early retirees who have more than enough money but live like they're broke because they can't psychologically flip the switch from "save" to "spend." This is its own kind of failure – you sacrificed to build wealth, and now you're sacrificing again by not enjoying it.

A few things that help:

  • Run the numbers regularly. Seeing that you're on track gives permission to spend.
  • Create a "fun money" category that you commit to spending. Not optional.
  • Remember why you did this. The point was freedom, not a high score on your brokerage statement.
  • Consider a financial advisor who can provide objective validation that you're okay.

The Goal

Early retirement isn't about escaping work – it's about having the freedom to do what matters to you. Make sure you actually use that freedom. The perfect portfolio means nothing if you never enjoy it.

The transition from working to early retirement is one of the biggest shifts you'll make. Plan the money carefully, but don't neglect the life part. Both matter.
