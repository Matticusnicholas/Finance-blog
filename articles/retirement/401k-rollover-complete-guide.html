The Complete 401(k) Rollover Guide: What To Do When You Leave Your Job

January 22, 2026 · 13 min read

Every time you change jobs, you face a decision about your 401(k) that could cost – or save – you tens of thousands of dollars over time. And most people just... don't think about it. They leave the money sitting in their old employer's plan, or worse, cash it out.

I've changed jobs four times. Each time, I had to figure out what to do with the old 401(k). The first time, I did nothing and forgot about it for three years (dumb). The second time, I made a mistake that triggered an unexpected tax bill. By the third and fourth time, I finally had a system.

Let me save you from my learning curve.

Your Four Options (And When Each Makes Sense)

Option 1: Leave it in your old employer's plan

When this makes sense:

  • Your old plan has excellent, low-cost investment options
  • You have access to institutional share classes you can't get in an IRA
  • You're between 55-59½ and might need the money (special early withdrawal rules)
  • You're being sued or have creditor concerns (401(k)s have stronger protection than IRAs in some states)

Downsides:

  • You'll eventually forget about it (I had three orphan accounts at one point)
  • Limited investment options – usually 15-25 funds
  • No control over plan changes
  • Can't add more money to it

Option 2: Roll it into your new employer's 401(k)

When this makes sense:

  • Your new plan is excellent with low fees
  • You want to keep everything in one place
  • You might do a "mega backdoor Roth" and need to avoid pro-rata issues
  • You want the creditor protection of a 401(k)

Downsides:

  • Still limited to whatever funds your new employer offers
  • Many plans have waiting periods before you can roll money in
  • If your new plan is bad, you're making things worse

Option 3: Roll it into an IRA

When this makes sense:

  • You want more investment options (stocks, ETFs, bonds, etc.)
  • Your 401(k) plans have mediocre options or high fees
  • You want professional management
  • You're consolidating multiple old 401(k)s

Downsides:

  • If you have pre-tax IRA money and do backdoor Roth conversions, you'll hit pro-rata rule issues
  • Less creditor protection than 401(k) in some states
  • No loan option (401(k)s sometimes allow loans)

Option 4: Cash it out (Almost never do this)

When this makes sense: Almost never. Maybe if you're facing bankruptcy or have a genuine emergency with no other options.

Why it's terrible:

  • You'll pay income tax on the entire amount
  • 10% early withdrawal penalty if you're under 59½
  • You lose decades of tax-advantaged growth

A $50,000 401(k) cashed out at age 35 might net you $32,000 after taxes and penalties. That same $50,000 left to grow could be worth $400,000+ by retirement. You're not just losing $50,000 – you're losing $400,000.

The Hidden Cost of Cashing Out

Studies show that about 40% of workers cash out their 401(k) when changing jobs. This is one of the biggest wealth destroyers in America. Don't be in that 40%.

Why I Rolled Mine Into a Managed IRA

After my last job change, I consolidated three old 401(k)s into a single IRA. Here's my reasoning:

  1. My old plans were mediocre. High expense ratios on the funds, limited options, annoying interfaces.
  2. I wanted professional management. Not because I can't pick index funds myself, but because I wanted someone actively managing for tax efficiency.
  3. Consolidation = simplicity. One account to track instead of four scattered across different providers.

What sold me was their approach to tax management. With a 401(k), you're stuck with whatever the plan offers. With their managed IRA, they can be strategic about which positions to sell, when to harvest losses, and how to minimize the tax drag on my portfolio. Over a 20-30 year timeline, that tax efficiency adds up to real money.

The founder built her own proprietary investment models, and her son has developed a more modern approach – his strategy was up over 40% in 2025, which obviously won't happen every year, but it shows they're not just putting you in a generic target-date fund and forgetting about you.

They typically work with clients who have $1 million or more in investable assets, so if your 401(k) is $50,000, they're probably not the right fit. But if you've accumulated significant retirement savings across multiple jobs and want it managed professionally with a focus on tax efficiency, they're worth talking to. Especially if you're in the Pinellas County or Tampa Bay area and want someone local.

How to Actually Execute a Rollover

The mechanics matter. Do this wrong and you'll trigger taxes and penalties.

Direct rollover (the right way)

The money goes directly from your old plan to your new account. You never touch it. The check is made out to the new custodian "for the benefit of" (FBO) you.

Example: "Fidelity Investments FBO John Smith"

No taxes. No penalties. No withholding. This is what you want.

Indirect rollover (the risky way)

The money comes to you first, then you deposit it into the new account within 60 days.

Problems with this approach:

  • Your old plan will withhold 20% for taxes, so you receive less than the full amount
  • You have to come up with that 20% from other funds to roll over the full amount
  • If you miss the 60-day deadline, the whole thing becomes a taxable distribution
  • You can only do one indirect rollover per 12-month period

Just do a direct rollover. There's no reason to complicate this.

Step-by-step process

  1. Open your new IRA (or confirm your new 401(k) accepts rollovers)
  2. Get the receiving account's information – account number, mailing address, any specific instructions
  3. Contact your old 401(k) provider – request a "direct rollover" to your new account
  4. Complete their paperwork – they'll have forms for this
  5. Specify how to handle the check – make sure it's payable to the new custodian, not you
  6. Follow up – these things take 2-4 weeks typically. Check that the money arrives.

Traditional vs. Roth: What Rolls Where

This trips people up:

  • Traditional 401(k) → Traditional IRA (no tax impact)
  • Roth 401(k) → Roth IRA (no tax impact)
  • Traditional 401(k) → Roth IRA (taxable conversion!)

If you want to convert traditional to Roth, you can, but you'll owe income tax on the entire converted amount. This might make sense if you're in a low tax bracket temporarily, but don't do it accidentally.

The Pro-Rata Rule Trap

If you do backdoor Roth IRA contributions (contribute to traditional IRA, convert to Roth), having pre-tax IRA money creates a mess called the pro-rata rule.

Basically, the IRS looks at ALL your traditional IRA money and calculates what percentage is pre-tax vs. after-tax. You can't just convert the after-tax portion – you have to convert a proportional mix.

If you're doing backdoor Roth contributions, you might want to:

  • Roll old 401(k)s into your current 401(k) instead of an IRA
  • Or accept that backdoor Roth is off the table

This is a relatively advanced issue, but worth knowing about.

Company Stock Complications (NUA)

If your 401(k) holds company stock, there's a special rule called Net Unrealized Appreciation (NUA) that might save you money.

The short version: instead of rolling company stock into an IRA, you might benefit from transferring it to a taxable brokerage account. You'd pay ordinary income tax on the original cost basis, but all the appreciation gets taxed at long-term capital gains rates when you eventually sell.

This can save significant money if your company stock has appreciated a lot. But the rules are complex – get professional help if this applies to you.

Timeline: Don't Wait Forever

There's no legal deadline to roll over your 401(k) after leaving a job, but don't procrastinate:

  • Some plans force out small balances (under $5,000)
  • Old employers get acquired, plans change, things get complicated
  • You'll forget about it and lose track of the account

Aim to handle it within 90 days of leaving your job. Add it to your offboarding checklist.

What I'd Do Differently

Looking back on my 401(k) history:

  • I'd have consolidated sooner. Having money scattered across multiple accounts means suboptimal asset allocation and extra complexity.
  • I'd have moved to professional management earlier. The tax efficiency alone would have been worth it, and I probably would have made fewer emotional decisions during market volatility.
  • I'd have avoided the one time I did an indirect rollover. The 20% withholding created a short-term cash crunch that was totally unnecessary.

The Bottom Line

A 401(k) rollover is one of those things that seems complicated but is actually straightforward if you do it right. The key decisions: where to move it, and making sure you do a direct (not indirect) rollover. Get those right, and you're protecting decades of tax-advantaged growth.
