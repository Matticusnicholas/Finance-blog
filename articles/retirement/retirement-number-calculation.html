How to Calculate Your Actual Retirement Number

December 1, 2025 · 14 min read

"You need $1 million to retire." You've probably heard something like this. It's a nice round number that sounds impressive. It's also completely meaningless without context.

Someone who spends $30,000/year has very different needs than someone who spends $100,000/year. Your retirement number depends on YOUR expected expenses, not some arbitrary milestone.

Let me show you how to actually calculate what you need.

The 4% Rule: The Foundation

Most retirement planning starts with the "4% rule," which comes from the Trinity Study. The basic idea: if you withdraw 4% of your portfolio in the first year of retirement, then adjust for inflation each subsequent year, your money has historically lasted 30 years about 95% of the time.

This gives us a simple formula:

The Basic Formula

Retirement Number = Annual Expenses × 25

(Because 4% × 25 = 100%)

If you need $40,000/year: $40,000 × 25 = $1,000,000

If you need $60,000/year: $60,000 × 25 = $1,500,000

If you need $80,000/year: $80,000 × 25 = $2,000,000

That's the quick version. But let's dig deeper, because "annual expenses" isn't as simple as it sounds.

Step 1: Estimate Your Retirement Expenses

This is where most people go wrong. They either:

  • Use their current expenses without adjusting for retirement changes
  • Use vague rules like "you'll need 70-80% of your current income"

Neither approach is very accurate. Here's what I recommend instead:

Start with your current budget

Look at what you actually spend now. If you don't have a budget, look at your bank statements for the last year. Add up all your expenses.

Remove expenses that will disappear

  • Mortgage payment (if you'll have it paid off)
  • Retirement contributions (you're done saving!)
  • Commuting costs
  • Work-related expenses (clothes, lunches, etc.)
  • College costs for kids (if applicable)

Add expenses that will increase

  • Healthcare (often the biggest one – budget generously)
  • Travel (if that's part of your retirement plan)
  • Hobbies and leisure
  • Home maintenance (you'll be around more to notice things)

Example calculation

Category Current Retirement Est.
Housing (mortgage/rent) $1,800 $0 (paid off)
Property tax/insurance $400 $500
Utilities $250 $300
Healthcare $200 $800
Food $600 $650
Transportation $500 $300
Entertainment/Travel $400 $800
Personal/Misc $300 $300
Retirement savings $1,000 $0
Total $5,450/mo $3,650/mo

Annual retirement expenses: $3,650 × 12 = $43,800

Retirement number: $43,800 × 25 = $1,095,000

Step 2: Account for Social Security

Social Security will cover part of your expenses. You can estimate your benefit at ssa.gov.

Let's say your estimated Social Security benefit is $2,000/month ($24,000/year). That means your portfolio only needs to cover the remainder:

$43,800 - $24,000 = $19,800/year from portfolio

Retirement number: $19,800 × 25 = $495,000

See how different that is from "$1 million"? Social Security changes the math significantly.

Social Security Caveats

  • Benefits may be reduced in the future (plan conservatively)
  • Taking benefits before full retirement age reduces your payment
  • Delaying past full retirement age increases your payment (up to age 70)

Step 3: Consider Other Income Sources

Do you have:

  • A pension?
  • Rental income?
  • Part-time work plans?
  • Annuity income?

Any of these reduce what your portfolio needs to provide.

If you have a $1,500/month pension plus Social Security:

$43,800 - $24,000 (SS) - $18,000 (pension) = $1,800/year from portfolio

Retirement number: $1,800 × 25 = $45,000

Obviously, most people don't have pensions these days. But if you do, it dramatically changes your needs.

Step 4: Adjust for Inflation (Optional but Smart)

If you're far from retirement, you need to account for the fact that expenses will be higher when you retire due to inflation.

At 3% annual inflation:

  • $43,800 today = $59,000 in 10 years
  • $43,800 today = $79,000 in 20 years
  • $43,800 today = $106,000 in 30 years

So if you're 35 and planning to retire at 65, you'd need: $106,000 × 25 = $2.65 million (not accounting for Social Security, which also adjusts for inflation).

This seems scary, but remember: your savings are also growing during this time. Compound growth typically outpaces inflation if you're invested appropriately.

Step 5: Run the Numbers Multiple Ways

I like to calculate three scenarios:

Conservative scenario

Higher expenses, lower Social Security, 3.5% withdrawal rate instead of 4%. This is my "be safe" number.

Expected scenario

Best estimates for everything using the 4% rule. This is my target.

Optimistic scenario

Lower expenses, higher Social Security, maybe some part-time income. This is my "I could technically retire early" number.

Having all three helps me understand the range and not fixate on a single number that might be wrong.

The 4% Rule: Limitations

The 4% rule is useful but not perfect. Some considerations:

It assumes a 30-year retirement

If you retire at 40, you might need your money to last 50+ years. A 3.5% or 3% withdrawal rate is more appropriate.

It's based on historical US market returns

Future returns might be different (lower, potentially). Some experts now recommend 3.5% to be safer.

Spending isn't constant in retirement

Most retirees spend more early (the "go-go years"), less in the middle ("slow-go years"), and then more at the end (healthcare in "no-go years"). The 4% rule assumes constant inflation-adjusted spending.

It doesn't account for flexibility

If you can reduce spending during market downturns, your success rate goes up dramatically. The 4% rule assumes rigid withdrawals regardless of market conditions.

My Personal Calculation (Example)

Here's how I think about my own retirement number:

Expected annual expenses in retirement: $50,000

Expected Social Security (conservative estimate): $20,000

Portfolio needs to provide: $30,000/year

Using 4% rule: $30,000 × 25 = $750,000

Using more conservative 3.5% rule: $30,000 × 28.5 = $857,000

My target: $900,000 (gives me a cushion)

Notice this is less than $1 million. My lifestyle doesn't require $1 million. Yours might require more or less. That's the whole point – it depends on your life.

What to Do With Your Number

Once you have your number:

  1. Calculate how much you need to save monthly to get there. There are online calculators for this. Factor in what you already have saved and how many years until retirement.
  2. Adjust if the monthly savings seems impossible. Either save more, plan to work longer, or reduce expected expenses.
  3. Revisit this calculation every few years. Your expenses, expected Social Security, and timeline will change. Update your number accordingly.

The Bottom Line

Forget arbitrary milestones like "$1 million." Your retirement number is personal and should be based on:

  1. Your expected annual expenses in retirement
  2. Social Security and any other guaranteed income
  3. A safe withdrawal rate (4% for 30 years, less for longer retirements)

For many people, the actual number needed is lower than they think. For some high spenders, it's higher. But at least now you're working with YOUR number, not some generic advice that doesn't fit your life.

Quick Calculation

Step 1: Estimate annual retirement expenses

Step 2: Subtract expected Social Security

Step 3: Multiply by 25 (for 4% rule) or 28-33 (for more conservative planning)

That's your retirement number.
