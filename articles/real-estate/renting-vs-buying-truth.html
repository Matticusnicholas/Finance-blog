Renting vs Buying: The Math Nobody Shows You

January 8, 2026 · 15 min read

"Renting is throwing money away."

"Buying is always a good investment."

"You're paying someone else's mortgage when you rent."

I've heard all of these. They're usually wrong, or at least wildly oversimplified. The rent vs. buy decision is math, not morality. Let me show you how to actually think about it.

The Oversimplified Argument for Buying

The standard argument goes like this: "If you pay $2,000/month in rent, that's $24,000/year gone. If you pay $2,000/month in mortgage, at least you're building equity!"

This sounds logical but ignores several important things:

  1. Most of your early mortgage payments are interest, not equity
  2. Homeownership has significant additional costs
  3. The opportunity cost of your down payment matters
  4. Transaction costs are substantial

Let's dig into each of these.

Where Your Mortgage Payment Actually Goes

Take a $400,000 home with 20% down ($80,000). You're financing $320,000 at 7% for 30 years. Monthly payment: $2,129 (principal and interest only).

In your first year, here's roughly how those payments break down:

Month Total Payment Principal Interest % to Interest
Month 1 $2,129 $263 $1,867 87.6%
Month 12 $2,129 $284 $1,845 86.7%
Year 1 Total $25,548 $3,240 $22,308 87.3%

In year one, 87% of your mortgage payment – over $22,000 – is interest. That's money that doesn't build equity. That money is just as "gone" as rent.

You only built about $3,240 in equity from payments. Add property appreciation (if any), and you might be in decent shape. But the "every dollar builds equity" argument is simply false for the first many years.

The True Cost of Owning

Your mortgage payment is just the beginning. Here are the costs that homeownership brings that renters don't pay:

Property taxes

On a $400,000 home, this might be $4,000-12,000/year depending on location. Let's say $6,000/year = $500/month.

Homeowners insurance

Typically $1,200-3,000/year. Let's say $1,800/year = $150/month.

Maintenance

The standard rule is 1-2% of home value per year. On a $400,000 home, that's $4,000-8,000/year = $333-667/month. And yes, these costs happen – roofs need replacing, HVAC breaks down, pipes leak.

PMI (if less than 20% down)

Could be $100-300/month until you hit 20% equity.

HOA fees (if applicable)

$200-600/month is common in many areas.

Let's add this up for our $400,000 home example:

Cost Monthly
Principal & Interest $2,129
Property Tax $500
Insurance $150
Maintenance (1.5%) $500
Total $3,279

And of that $3,279, only $263/month (month 1) is actually building equity. The rest is either interest or non-equity costs. That's $3,016/month in "gone" money – potentially more than renting a similar place.

The Down Payment Opportunity Cost

That $80,000 down payment could have been invested in the stock market instead. Historically, the stock market returns about 7% after inflation. $80,000 invested at 7% for 10 years becomes about $157,000.

By putting that money in a house instead, you're betting that home appreciation plus equity building will beat what you'd make in the market. Sometimes it does. Sometimes it doesn't. It's not a guaranteed win.

Transaction Costs: The Hidden Killer

When you buy a home:

  • Closing costs: 2-5% of purchase price ($8,000-20,000)
  • Inspection, appraisal, etc.: $500-1,500

When you sell a home:

  • Real estate commissions: 5-6% ($20,000-24,000 on a $400,000 home)
  • Closing costs: 1-3% ($4,000-12,000)
  • Repairs/staging to sell: varies ($5,000+)

Total transaction costs to buy and later sell: easily $40,000-60,000 or more.

This means you need significant appreciation just to break even. If you buy and sell within 3-5 years, these costs often eat any gains.

When Buying Wins

All that said, buying can still be the right choice. Here's when:

You'll stay 7+ years

This gives you time for appreciation to outpace transaction costs and for your equity percentage to grow. The longer you stay, the more buying tends to make sense.

Rent is high relative to buying costs

In some markets, renting is so expensive that buying makes sense even with all the extra costs. The "price to rent ratio" in your area matters a lot.

You value stability and control

Owning means no landlord can kick you out, raise your rent, or refuse to let you paint the walls. This has value even if it doesn't show up in the math.

You're disciplined about not overbuying

If you buy a modest home and would otherwise rent something similar, the math often works out. If buying tempts you to get a bigger, nicer place than you'd rent, that changes the equation.

Local market conditions favor buying

Some markets have better rent-to-buy economics than others. Research your specific area.

When Renting Wins

You might move in less than 5 years

Transaction costs make short-term ownership expensive. If there's a reasonable chance you'll relocate, rent.

You want to invest the difference

If renting costs less than owning (which it often does), you can invest the difference. Historically, this has often produced better returns than home equity, with more liquidity.

You don't want maintenance responsibilities

Something breaks? Call the landlord. This peace of mind has value.

The local market is overheated

In some cities, buying makes almost no financial sense at current prices. It's okay to wait or rent indefinitely in these markets.

How to Run the Numbers

Here's the basic framework I use to compare renting vs buying:

Monthly cost of buying:

  • Mortgage payment (P&I)
  • + Property taxes
  • + Insurance
  • + PMI (if applicable)
  • + Maintenance (1-2% of home value annually, divided by 12)
  • + HOA (if applicable)
  • - Tax savings from mortgage interest deduction (if you itemize)
  • - Principal payment (this is equity, not a cost)
  • = True monthly cost of ownership

Monthly cost of renting:

  • Monthly rent
  • + Renter's insurance (~$15-30/month)
  • = True monthly cost of renting

Then factor in:

  • Expected appreciation (or depreciation) of the home
  • Return you'd get investing the down payment elsewhere
  • Transaction costs if you plan to sell
  • How long you plan to stay

There are good online calculators that do this math (NYT's "Is It Better to Rent or Buy" is excellent).

My Personal Take

I currently rent, even though I could afford to buy. In my market (expensive city), the math just doesn't work for buying right now. My rent is lower than what ownership would cost, I invest the difference, and I'm not sure I'll be here for 7+ years.

This doesn't make me feel bad or like a failure. It's just math. Renting isn't "throwing money away" – it's paying for housing flexibility and freedom from maintenance responsibilities.

The "throwing money away" argument also ignores that much of homeownership costs are equally "thrown away" – interest, taxes, insurance, and maintenance don't build equity either.

The Bottom Line

Neither renting nor buying is universally better. It depends on:

  • Your local market's rent-to-buy ratio
  • How long you plan to stay
  • Current interest rates
  • Your financial discipline
  • Your personal preferences and life situation

Run the actual numbers for your situation. Don't let your parents or society pressure you into buying if the math doesn't work. And don't assume renting is always smarter if buying makes sense in your market.

It's math, not morality. Do the math.

Quick Rule of Thumb

The "price to rent ratio": Divide the home price by annual rent for a similar place. If the ratio is under 15, buying often makes sense. If it's over 20, renting often makes more sense. Between 15-20, it depends on other factors.

Example: $400,000 home vs. $24,000/year rent = ratio of 16.7 (borderline, do detailed analysis)
