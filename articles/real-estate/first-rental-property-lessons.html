What I Learned From My First Rental Property (The Hard Way)

December 18, 2025 · 16 min read

After years of reading about real estate investing, I finally bought my first rental property. I thought I was prepared. I'd read all the books, listened to all the podcasts, run all the numbers.

I still made a bunch of expensive mistakes. Here's what actually happened and what I wish I'd known.

The Property

I bought a small 3-bedroom house in a working-class neighborhood. Purchase price: $165,000. I put 25% down ($41,250) and financed the rest. My plan was to rent it for $1,400/month.

On paper, the numbers looked great:

Item Monthly
Rent $1,400
Mortgage (P&I) -$660
Property Tax -$180
Insurance -$90
Vacancy (5%) -$70
Maintenance (5%) -$70
Cash Flow $330

$330/month cash flow. 10% cash-on-cash return. I was going to be a real estate mogul.

Reality had other plans.

Lesson 1: Your Renovation Estimates Are Wrong

The house needed "some work" before renting. In my optimistic mind, this meant fresh paint, some minor repairs, maybe $5,000.

The actual list:

  • New flooring throughout (carpet was disgusting): $4,200
  • Interior paint: $2,800
  • Fix plumbing issues: $1,400
  • Electrical updates: $900
  • New appliances (existing ones were ancient): $1,800
  • HVAC repair: $800
  • Landscaping cleanup: $400
  • Various other things: $700

Total: $13,000 – more than double my estimate.

Every experienced investor told me to budget 20-30% more than I thought for rehab. I didn't listen. They were right.

The Rule

Whatever you think repairs will cost, multiply by 1.5. Then add a 20% contingency on top of that. You'll still probably go over.

Lesson 2: Vacancy Lasts Longer Than Expected

My spreadsheet had 5% vacancy – about 2.5 weeks per year. The reality of my first year:

  • 8 weeks of renovation before first tenant
  • First tenant lasted 6 months, then broke lease
  • 4 weeks vacant while finding second tenant

That's 12+ weeks of vacancy in year one. Not 2.5 weeks. Actual vacancy rate: 23%.

To be fair, the first 8 weeks were renovation, not traditional vacancy. But it's still time the property wasn't generating income while I was paying the mortgage.

Lesson 3: Property Management Eats Your Cash Flow

I started out self-managing. After all, one property – how hard could it be?

Here's what self-managing involved:

  • Middle-of-the-night calls about HVAC issues
  • Showing the property to prospective tenants (usually no-shows)
  • Dealing with maintenance requests
  • Learning landlord-tenant law in my state
  • Collecting rent (awkward when they're late)
  • Coordinating repairs

After 8 months, I hired a property manager. They charge 10% of rent ($140/month) plus half the first month's rent for placing new tenants.

That $330/month cash flow? Now it's $190/month. Still positive, but not the goldmine I imagined.

Lesson 4: Maintenance Never Stops

Year one maintenance costs beyond the initial renovation:

  • Garbage disposal replacement: $180
  • Clogged drain (turned out to be roots): $350
  • HVAC tune-up: $120
  • Small roof repair: $400
  • Various minor things: $300

That's $1,350, or about $112/month. My spreadsheet assumed 5% of rent ($70/month). Real number: 8%.

And this was a GOOD year. No major systems failed. In year 3, the water heater died ($1,200 replacement). In year 4, I had to replace a significant portion of the HVAC system ($3,800).

The BiggerPockets crowd talks about budgeting 8-12% for maintenance on older properties. They're right. 5% is too optimistic.

Lesson 5: Tenant Selection Is Everything

My first tenant looked fine on paper: steady job, decent credit, no evictions. What the application didn't show: she had a boyfriend who wasn't on the lease and who turned out to be the actual problem.

He moved in unofficially. Loud parties. Complaints from neighbors. Damage to the property. When I tried to enforce lease terms, she gave notice and left – leaving $2,000 in damage that her security deposit didn't fully cover.

My second tenant, who I screened more carefully, has been there for 3 years now. Pays on time. Takes care of the property. Night and day difference.

What I do now:

  • Require income of 3x rent (verified with pay stubs and employer contact)
  • Actually call previous landlords and ask specific questions
  • Run credit AND background check
  • Meet prospective tenants in person when possible
  • Trust my gut – if something feels off, pass

Lesson 6: Location Within a Location Matters

The neighborhood was "B-class" – working class but not rough. What I didn't realize was that my specific street was worse than the neighborhood average. Slightly higher crime. Less owner-occupied homes. More turnover.

This affected tenant quality and required me to price slightly below neighborhood comps. A house two streets over with worse finishes gets $100/month more because of the specific block.

I should have driven the neighborhood at different times – morning, evening, weekend. Talked to neighbors. Looked up crime stats for the specific block, not just the zip code.

Lesson 7: The Real Numbers

After three years, here's my actual performance versus projections:

Metric Projected Actual
Monthly rent $1,400 $1,450 (raised in Y2)
Vacancy rate 5% 9% average
Maintenance 5% 11% average
Property management 0% (self) 10%
Monthly cash flow $330 $75 average

$75/month cash flow. On a $54,000 investment (down payment plus renovation). That's a 1.7% cash-on-cash return.

The stock market would have done better.

But Wait – It's Not All Bad

Here's what the cash flow numbers don't show:

Equity building

Each mortgage payment builds equity. Over 3 years, I've paid down about $7,500 in principal. That's wealth accumulation even when cash flow is low.

Appreciation

The property has appreciated about 15% in 3 years. On paper, that's about $25,000 in equity gain (minus transaction costs if I sold).

Tax benefits

Depreciation deductions have sheltered my rental income from taxes. I'm actually showing a loss on paper most years while making positive cash flow.

Rent increases

As rents rise and my mortgage stays fixed, cash flow improves. In year 3, I'm actually close to my original projections.

When I factor in everything – cash flow, equity paydown, appreciation, tax benefits – the total return is actually pretty good. Just not as good as I projected, and not as easy as I expected.

Would I Do It Again?

Yes, but differently. Here's what I'd change:

  1. More conservative projections. Assume 10% vacancy, 10% maintenance, property management from day one.
  2. Better property inspection. Hire a good inspector AND get contractor estimates before buying.
  3. Hyper-focus on location. The specific street matters. Walk it. Drive it at night.
  4. Screen tenants obsessively. It's worth waiting for the right tenant rather than filling vacancy fast.
  5. Have bigger reserves. I started with 3 months of expenses. Should have been 6.

Advice for First-Time Rental Investors

  • Run the numbers conservatively. Assume everything will cost more and take longer.
  • Don't rely on appreciation. Your deal should work on cash flow alone. Appreciation is a bonus.
  • Have cash reserves. Things break. Tenants leave. Be prepared.
  • Understand your local laws. Landlord-tenant law varies hugely by state/city. Know your rights AND obligations.
  • Start small. A single-family or small multi-family lets you learn without catastrophic risk.
  • Join local investor groups. The knowledge from experienced investors is invaluable.
  • Be patient. Real estate wealth builds slowly. Don't expect to get rich on your first property.

Real estate can absolutely build wealth. But it's not the passive income dream that gurus sell. It's a business that requires capital, time, knowledge, and patience.

Go in with realistic expectations and you'll be fine. Go in thinking you'll get rich quick and you'll be disappointed at best, broke at worst.

The Bottom Line

My first rental property was a good learning experience and will likely end up being a decent investment. But it was harder, more expensive, and less profitable than I expected. Use conservative numbers, have cash reserves, and be prepared to work. "Passive income" is a myth – at least at the beginning.
