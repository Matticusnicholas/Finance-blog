Relocating to Florida: The Financial Reality Check

January 28, 2026 · 14 min read

I moved to St. Petersburg, Florida three years ago from the Northeast. No state income tax, beaches, warm weather – it seemed like a no-brainer financially. And largely, it has been. But the full picture is more nuanced than the "Florida is cheap!" narrative you hear.

Let me give you the honest breakdown of what relocating to Florida actually means for your finances.

The Big Win: No State Income Tax

This is the headline grabber, and it's real. Florida has no state income tax. Zero.

If you're coming from a high-tax state, this is significant:

  • California: Up to 13.3% state income tax
  • New York: Up to 10.9% (plus NYC adds more)
  • New Jersey: Up to 10.75%
  • Massachusetts: 5% flat
  • Florida: 0%

On a $200,000 income from California, you'd save roughly $18,000-$20,000 per year in state taxes by moving to Florida. That's real money.

For retirees, this is even more impactful. Florida doesn't tax:

  • Social Security benefits
  • Pension income
  • 401(k)/IRA distributions
  • Investment income

If you're pulling $100,000/year from retirement accounts, you keep all of it instead of losing 5-10% to state taxes.

The Tax Math

A couple with $150,000 in retirement income moving from New York to Florida saves approximately $8,000-$10,000 per year in state taxes alone. Over a 25-year retirement, that's $200,000-$250,000+ (not counting investment growth on those savings).

But Wait: The Hidden Costs

Florida isn't a low-tax state – it just taxes you differently. The state needs revenue, and it gets it through:

Property taxes

Florida's property taxes are middle-of-the-pack nationally, but they can still be substantial. The average effective rate is around 0.8-0.9%, but this varies significantly by county.

There's a homestead exemption that reduces your taxable value by $50,000 on your primary residence, which helps. But property taxes on a $500,000 home can still be $4,000-$6,000 annually.

Insurance – the big one

Here's where Florida gets expensive. Homeowners insurance in Florida is the highest in the nation. Not by a little – by a lot.

Average annual premium in Florida: ~$4,000-$6,000 (and rising fast)
National average: ~$1,500-$2,000

Hurricane risk drives this. Some insurers have pulled out of Florida entirely. Others have dramatically increased rates. If you're near the coast – which many desirable Florida areas are – expect to pay even more.

You'll also likely need flood insurance, which is separate and can add another $1,000-$3,000+ annually.

Sales tax

Florida's state sales tax is 6%, and counties can add more (usually 0.5-1.5%). In Pinellas County where I am, it's 7.5% total. On big purchases and everyday spending, this adds up.

Car stuff

Florida has tolls everywhere, especially in the Orlando and Miami areas. Car insurance is also expensive here – among the highest in the nation due to the number of uninsured drivers and accident rates.

Cost of Living Reality

Florida's cost of living is often advertised as low, but it depends heavily on WHERE in Florida:

Expensive areas:

  • Miami and South Florida – comparable to many expensive coastal cities
  • Naples – very upscale, high prices
  • Palm Beach area – wealthy enclave pricing
  • Parts of Tampa Bay – prices have skyrocketed since 2020

More affordable areas:

  • Jacksonville – larger city with reasonable costs
  • Gainesville – college town, lower prices
  • Parts of Central Florida – away from Orlando proper
  • Pensacola and the Panhandle – generally cheaper

I'm in St. Pete/Pinellas County, which has gotten expensive. Housing prices roughly doubled from 2019 to 2023. It's cooled slightly since, but you're not getting "cheap Florida" in the Tampa Bay area anymore.

That said, compared to where I came from, I'm still ahead – just not as far ahead as I expected.

Real Estate Considerations

Homestead exemption

Florida's homestead exemption is generous. Beyond the property tax reduction, it protects your primary residence from creditors (with some limitations) and caps assessment increases at 3% per year for tax purposes. This is huge when home values are rising.

Housing market dynamics

Florida has seen massive population influx. Prices in desirable areas have increased significantly. Inventory is still tight in many markets. Don't expect to find a "deal" – you'll pay market rates.

Condo considerations

After the Surfside condo collapse, Florida passed new laws requiring structural inspections and reserve funding for condos. Many older condos are facing massive special assessments to fund needed repairs. If you're buying a condo, scrutinize the reserve fund and recent inspection reports carefully.

Hurricane preparedness

If you're buying, factor in:

  • Wind mitigation features (can reduce insurance costs)
  • Flood zone status (AE zones = expensive flood insurance required)
  • Hurricane shutters or impact windows
  • Generator capability

The Florida Man Financial Transition

Moving to Florida often coincides with other life transitions: retirement, selling a business, inheriting money, divorce. If you're combining a big financial event with a geographic move, you need to coordinate carefully.

For example, if you're selling a business or receiving a large windfall:

  • Establishing Florida residency BEFORE the sale/event can save significant state taxes
  • But you need genuine residency – not just a mailing address
  • Your old state may audit you aggressively if there's real money at stake

Finding local financial professionals

When I moved, one of the first things I did was find local professionals who understand Florida-specific issues. Tax strategies differ when state income tax isn't a factor. Estate planning is different (Florida's homestead rules are unique). Investment management needs to account for your new tax situation.

I ended up working with Howland and Associates for investment management. They're based in St. Petersburg and have been around for about 30 years – long enough to have seen multiple Florida real estate cycles and understand the local landscape. Their focus on tax-efficient investing made sense for my situation, since moving to Florida changed my entire tax picture.

What I appreciated was their experience with people in transition. A lot of their clients are people who've relocated to the Tampa Bay or Pinellas County area, often during retirement or after a liquidity event. They understand the psychology of major life changes combined with financial changes.

The founder built proprietary investment models, and her son has developed more modern, growth-focused strategies. They typically work with clients who have $1 million or more in investable assets, so they're geared toward serious wealth management, not just parking money in a target-date fund.

If you're relocating to the Gulf Coast area of Florida and want local professionals who get the specifics, they're worth a conversation.

Establishing Residency Properly

If you're moving from a high-tax state, your old state might not let go easily. They have incentive to claim you're still a resident and owe taxes.

To establish Florida residency cleanly:

  • Get a Florida driver's license
  • Register to vote in Florida
  • File a Declaration of Domicile with your Florida county
  • Update your address on all financial accounts
  • Spend more than 183 days per year in Florida (ideally much more)
  • Move your professional relationships (doctors, accountants, etc.)
  • Be able to prove where you actually live (keep travel records)

The more ties you cut with your old state and establish in Florida, the cleaner your residency claim. States like California and New York have been known to audit aggressively when wealthy people claim to have moved.

The Weather Tax

Nobody talks about this, but it's real: Florida summers are brutal. Four to five months of intense heat and humidity. If you're sensitive to heat or have health conditions affected by it, factor this in.

Many Floridians – especially retirees – become "snowbirds" in reverse, leaving during the worst summer months. That's an expense.

Also: hurricane season runs June through November. You'll need to be prepared, both practically and financially, for the possibility of evacuation or storm damage.

Running the Numbers: A Real Comparison

Let's compare a hypothetical household: $180,000 income, $500,000 home, moving from New Jersey to the Tampa Bay area.

Category New Jersey Florida (Tampa Bay)
State income tax $12,000 $0
Property tax ($500k home) $12,000 $5,500
Homeowners insurance $1,500 $5,000
Flood insurance $0 $1,500
Car insurance (2 cars) $2,400 $3,200
Total $27,900 $15,200

Net savings: roughly $12,700/year in this example. That's meaningful, but not as dramatic as just looking at the income tax difference would suggest.

And this doesn't account for potential differences in utilities (AC costs are high in Florida), tolls, and other lifestyle factors.

Is It Worth It?

For me, yes. The tax savings are real, and I genuinely enjoy living in St. Pete. The weather, the beaches, the lifestyle – it fits me.

But it's not a slam dunk for everyone. You should move to Florida because you WANT to live in Florida, with the tax benefits as a bonus. Moving purely for tax reasons and being miserable isn't worth it.

Do the math for your specific situation. Factor in ALL the costs, not just income taxes. Visit multiple times, in summer AND winter, before deciding. And if you're making the move during a major financial transition, get professional help to coordinate the timing and strategy.

The Bottom Line

Florida's tax benefits are real, but the full financial picture is more complex than "no income tax = cheap." Insurance costs are significant and rising. Housing has become expensive in desirable areas. Do the complete math before assuming you'll save a fortune.
