How Much House Can You Actually Afford?

November 28, 2025 · 10 min read

Banks will approve you for way more house than you should buy. That's not conspiracy – they make money on bigger loans. Their approval amount is based on what you can technically pay, not what you can comfortably pay while also saving for retirement, taking vacations, and not being stressed about money constantly.

Let me show you how to figure out what you can actually afford.

The Bank's Perspective vs. Your Perspective

Banks typically use a 28/36 rule:

  • Your housing costs (mortgage, taxes, insurance) shouldn't exceed 28% of gross income
  • Your total debt payments shouldn't exceed 36% of gross income

Some lenders will go higher, especially for borrowers with good credit or FHA loans. I've seen people approved with housing costs at 35%+ of gross income.

Here's the problem: 28% of GROSS income is actually a much bigger percentage of your take-home pay. If you make $100,000 gross, you might take home $70,000 after taxes and deductions. 28% of gross ($28,000) is actually 40% of take-home ($28,000 / $70,000).

Spending 40% of your take-home pay on housing leaves very little room for everything else: retirement savings, emergencies, lifestyle, other goals.

A More Conservative Approach

Instead of the bank's formula, I use these guidelines:

  • Total housing cost under 25% of gross income (better: under 20%)
  • Total housing cost under 30% of take-home pay (better: under 25%)
  • Mortgage balance under 3x annual gross income (better: under 2.5x)

These are more conservative than what banks allow, but they leave room for life to happen.

The True Cost of Homeownership

Remember, your mortgage payment isn't the whole picture. True monthly housing cost includes:

  • Principal and interest (the mortgage payment)
  • Property taxes (varies hugely by location, 0.5-2.5% of home value annually)
  • Homeowners insurance ($1,200-3,000+/year typical)
  • PMI (if down payment less than 20%)
  • HOA fees (if applicable)
  • Maintenance (1-2% of home value annually)
  • Utilities (often higher than apartments)

Let's do real math for a $400,000 house:

Cost Monthly
Mortgage (320K at 7%, 30yr) $2,129
Property tax (1.5%) $500
Insurance $175
Maintenance (1.5%) $500
Total $3,304

To afford $3,304/month at 25% of take-home, you'd need take-home pay of $13,216/month, or about $158,600/year. That roughly translates to gross income of $200,000+ depending on your tax situation.

Most people buying $400,000 houses don't make $200,000. They're stretching.

Working Backwards from Income

Here's a better approach: start with your income and work backwards to find your price range.

Example: $80,000 gross income

Take-home (estimated): $58,000/year or $4,833/month

Housing at 25% of take-home: $1,208/month

Subtracting taxes, insurance, and maintenance (estimated $600/month for a modest home), you can afford about $608/month for mortgage P&I.

At 7% interest, 30-year term, that supports a mortgage of about $91,000.

With 20% down, you could buy a home priced around $114,000.

With 10% down (plus PMI), maybe $105,000.

On $80,000 income, a comfortable, financially-sound home purchase is around $100,000-120,000.

In many markets, this is extremely limiting. In others, it's achievable. This is why location matters so much for financial health.

The Reality Check

I know what you're thinking: "There are no houses for $120,000 where I live."

You're probably right. Which means either:

  1. You need to increase income before buying
  2. You need to look at cheaper areas or housing types (condos, townhomes, further from city center)
  3. You need to save a larger down payment to reduce monthly costs
  4. You need to wait for prices or rates to become more favorable
  5. You accept stretching and being "house poor"

Option 5 is what most people do. It's not necessarily wrong – housing provides shelter and potential appreciation – but you should go in with eyes open about the tradeoffs.

Signs You're Buying Too Much House

  • You'll have to pause retirement contributions
  • Your emergency fund will be depleted by the down payment
  • You can't afford the payment if you lose your job
  • You're counting on future raises to make it comfortable
  • You're at the absolute max of bank approval
  • You feel stressed when you look at the monthly payment

If any of these apply, you might be overextending.

How I'd Approach It

If I were buying today, here's my process:

1. Calculate my true monthly budget

25-30% of take-home pay, max. This is all-in housing cost, not just mortgage.

2. Get pre-approved, but ignore the max amount

Pre-approval shows sellers you're serious. But shop well below what you're approved for.

3. Leave room for life

After housing, I want to still max out my retirement accounts, have an emergency fund, and enjoy some discretionary spending. If the house doesn't allow this, it's too expensive.

4. Buy less house than I can afford

You can always upgrade later. You can't easily downgrade when you're underwater or house-poor.

The Emotional Component

House buying is emotional. You fall in love with a place and suddenly the spreadsheet doesn't matter anymore. "We'll make it work" is a dangerous mindset.

Some tips:

  • Set your max price BEFORE you start looking. Write it down. Tell your agent.
  • Don't look at houses above your budget. You'll just torture yourself.
  • Sleep on any offer. The urgency is usually manufactured.
  • Remember: there's always another house. This one isn't your only chance.

The Bottom Line

Bank approval ≠ affordability. Banks will approve you for more than you should spend. Their job is to give loans; your job is to protect your financial future.

Be conservative. Buy less house than you can afford. Leave room in your budget for retirement savings, emergencies, and life.

Future you – the one not stressed about money, the one who can retire on time – will thank current you for not overextending on a house.

Quick Affordability Check

  • Total housing cost (PITI + maintenance) under 25% of gross income
  • Mortgage balance under 3x gross income
  • After buying, you can still max retirement accounts and maintain emergency fund

If all three are true, you can probably afford it comfortably.
