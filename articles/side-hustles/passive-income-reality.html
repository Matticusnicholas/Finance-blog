Passive Income: What's Real and What's BS

November 18, 2025 · 12 min read

"Make money while you sleep!" "Set it and forget it income!" "Fire your boss with passive income streams!"

The internet is full of passive income hype. But having tried several "passive" income strategies myself, I can tell you: most aren't as passive as advertised, and some are complete nonsense.

Let me break down what's real and what's marketing.

Defining "Passive" Realistically

Truly passive income – money that appears with zero ongoing effort – basically only comes from:

  1. Investment returns (dividends, interest, capital gains)
  2. Royalties from work you've already done (books, music, patents)
  3. Rent from property (though even this requires management)

Everything else sold as "passive income" is really "semi-passive" or "leveraged" income: you create something once and it continues to generate money, but it still requires maintenance, marketing, customer support, or ongoing work to sustain.

That doesn't mean these aren't worthwhile – they absolutely can be. But go in with realistic expectations.

Actually Passive: Investments

The most genuinely passive income is from investments. You buy, you hold, you collect returns.

Dividend stocks/funds

Buy dividend-paying stocks or funds like VYM or SCHD, and they'll pay you quarterly without any action from you.

Reality check: To generate meaningful income, you need significant capital. At a 3% dividend yield, you need $1,000,000 invested to generate $30,000/year. Most people don't have that sitting around.

Bond interest

Bonds pay interest regularly. Very passive.

Reality check: Current yields are decent (4-5%), but historically bonds have lower returns than stocks. And you still need significant capital for meaningful income.

Index fund growth

The S&P 500 has historically returned ~10%/year on average. That's passive growth you can eventually draw from.

Reality check: This is the best long-term wealth builder but isn't "income" until you sell. And selling means depleting your investment.

My Take

For most people, investment income is the end game – the goal you're building toward – not something you can start with unless you already have substantial assets.

Semi-Passive: Digital Products

Create once, sell repeatedly. Sounds great. Here's the reality:

Online courses

The promise: Create a course, put it on Udemy/Teachable/your own site, make money forever.

The reality: Creating a good course takes hundreds of hours. Then you need to market it constantly or it gets buried. Course platforms take significant cuts. You need to update content as things change. And the space is incredibly crowded.

I know people making good money from courses, but they treat it like a business, not a passive income stream. Probably 10-20+ hours/week ongoing.

Ebooks

The promise: Write a book, publish on Amazon, collect royalties.

The reality: Most self-published ebooks sell fewer than 100 copies. The ones that sell well have significant marketing efforts behind them. Royalties are often $1-3 per book, so you need serious volume.

After initial launch, it can be fairly passive if you have an audience. But building that audience is the hard part.

Templates, presets, digital downloads

The promise: Create a template/preset/printable once, sell it on Etsy or Gumroad.

The reality: Can work, especially in niches with dedicated audiences. But requires upfront marketing, customer support, and periodic updates. Competition is fierce in popular categories.

Semi-Passive: Content Creation

YouTube

The promise: Make videos, ads generate revenue, money flows while you sleep.

The reality: Building a YouTube channel to the point of meaningful income (monetization requires 1,000 subscribers + 4,000 watch hours) takes most people years of consistent effort. After that, you need to keep posting or the algorithm forgets you.

People earning $10K+/month on YouTube typically treat it as a full-time job with multiple uploads per week and extensive editing.

Blogging/Affiliate Marketing

The promise: Write blog posts, include affiliate links, get paid when people buy.

The reality: SEO is competitive and constantly changing. Building a blog to significant traffic takes years. Affiliate income is volatile – Amazon famously cut commissions drastically. And you need to keep creating content to maintain rankings.

I know bloggers earning well, but they're effectively running media businesses, not collecting passive checks.

Not Passive at All: Rental Properties

Rental income is often called passive, but as a rental property owner, I can tell you: it's not.

  • Finding and screening tenants
  • Handling maintenance requests
  • Dealing with repairs (or coordinating contractors)
  • Chasing late rent
  • Managing vacancies
  • Keeping up with landlord-tenant laws

Property management companies can handle most of this, but they take 8-10% of rent plus fees. That eats significantly into your returns.

Rental real estate can absolutely be a good investment, but calling it passive is misleading unless you have an expensive property manager.

Complete BS: Most "Passive Income" Schemes

Some things marketed as passive income are either scams or require so much work they're just regular jobs:

  • MLM/Network Marketing: The only people making money are the ones selling courses about making money. Run away.
  • Dropshipping "empires": Running a dropshipping business is a business. It requires constant marketing, customer service, and adaptation. Not passive.
  • "Automated" businesses: Every business requires attention. Automated systems still need monitoring and maintenance.
  • Crypto yield farming/staking: High yields come with high risk, and the space is rife with scams. Definitely not "set and forget."

What Actually Works Long-Term

Based on what I've seen and experienced:

Build assets over time

The most reliable passive income comes from accumulating assets: stocks, bonds, real estate. This takes years of saving and investing. No shortcuts.

Create with long-term value

Books, courses, and digital products CAN generate ongoing revenue, but think in terms of value that lasts. A well-written book on a timeless topic continues to sell. A course on last year's software gets outdated.

Understand the maintenance reality

Almost everything requires maintenance: websites need updating, courses need refreshing, real estate needs managing. Budget time for this or budget money for someone else to do it.

Focus on your main income first

The fastest path to passive income is often earning more in your career, investing the difference, and letting compound growth work. It's not sexy, but it's reliable.

My "Passive" Income Sources

For transparency, here's where my non-job income comes from:

  1. Investment returns: Dividends + growth from index funds. Truly passive. Required years of saving to build.
  2. Rental property: Net income after property management ~$200/month. Semi-passive (PM handles day-to-day, but I still deal with big decisions).
  3. Occasional freelance residuals: Some past projects pay ongoing royalties. Tiny amounts but truly passive.

Total "passive" income: maybe $600-800/month. Not enough to quit my job. But it compounds over time.

The Bottom Line

Real passive income is mostly about accumulating capital and letting it work for you through investments. Everything else typically requires either significant upfront effort, ongoing maintenance, or both.

Don't fall for get-rich-quick "passive income" schemes. Do focus on:

  • Building your career income
  • Investing consistently in index funds
  • If interested, creating assets (content, products) that can compound over time with reasonable maintenance

The goal is realistic: eventually, your investments generate enough that you don't HAVE to work. But getting there takes years of decidedly un-passive saving and investing.

Reality Check

If someone's selling you a "passive income system," they're making money selling you the system, not from the system itself. The truly wealthy don't sell courses on getting wealthy – they invest quietly and let compound growth do the work.
