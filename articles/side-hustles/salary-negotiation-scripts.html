Salary Negotiation: Scripts That Actually Work

December 5, 2025 · 11 min read

I've negotiated my salary six times across job offers and annual reviews. The first time, I was terrified and just accepted what they offered. The other five times, I used the strategies I'm about to share. Total extra money negotiated: about $47,000 over those five conversations.

Salary negotiation is one of the highest-value activities you can do for your financial life. A single successful negotiation can be worth tens of thousands of dollars over your career when you factor in percentage-based raises and future job offers that anchor to your current salary.

Here are the actual scripts and strategies that worked.

Fundamental Mindset Shifts

They expect you to negotiate

Hiring managers and HR people expect negotiation. They typically have room in the budget for it. The initial offer is rarely their best offer. When you don't negotiate, you're leaving money on the table that was already allocated for you.

You're not being greedy – you're being professional

Advocating for yourself is a normal part of business. Companies negotiate with vendors, clients, and partners. They expect employees to negotiate too. It's not personal.

The worst they can say is no

I've never heard of an offer being rescinded because someone politely negotiated. The worst outcome is they say "sorry, that's our best offer" and you're back where you started.

Before the Negotiation: Research

You need data to negotiate effectively:

Know your market value

Use multiple sources: Glassdoor, Levels.fyi (for tech), LinkedIn Salary, Payscale. Talk to people in similar roles. Know the range for your position, experience level, and location.

Know the company's range

Some states require companies to disclose salary ranges. Check job postings or ask HR directly. Knowing their range gives you a target.

Document your accomplishments

For reviews: list specific achievements, metrics, projects delivered. Quantify everything possible. "Increased sales by 15%" is better than "helped with sales."

Script 1: Responding to a Job Offer

They make an offer. Here's what to say:

"Thank you so much for this offer – I'm really excited about the opportunity to join [Company] and work on [specific thing you're excited about]. I'd like to take some time to review the details. Would it be okay if I got back to you by [reasonable date, usually 2-3 business days]?"

Why this works: Never accept or counter immediately. You want time to think. This is expected and professional.

Then, after reviewing:

"I've had a chance to review the offer and I'm very enthusiastic about the role. Based on my research into market rates for this position, and given my [X years of experience / specific relevant skills / etc.], I was hoping we could get closer to [$X]. Is there flexibility in the compensation?"

Why this works: You're enthusiastic (not threatening to walk away), you've done research (not just pulling numbers from nowhere), and you're asking openly (not demanding).

Script 2: When They Ask Your Salary Expectations First

The classic trap. They ask what you're looking for before making an offer. Options:

Option A: Deflect

"I'd prefer to learn more about the role and the full compensation package before discussing specific numbers. What's the budgeted range for this position?"

Option B: Give a range (if pressed)

"Based on my research for this type of role in this market, and given my experience level, I'm targeting something in the range of [$X to $Y]. But I'm flexible and want to understand the full picture including benefits, growth opportunities, and other factors."

Tip: Make your range's low end slightly above what you'd actually accept. They'll often anchor to the bottom of your range.

Script 3: Annual Review / Raise Request

Asking your current employer for more money requires a different approach:

"I'd like to talk about my compensation. Over the past year, I've [specific accomplishments with numbers where possible]. I've taken on [new responsibilities]. Based on market data and my contributions, I believe my compensation should be adjusted to [$X or X%]. Can we discuss this?"

Key elements:

  • Specific accomplishments (not "I've worked hard")
  • Market data reference (shows you've done research)
  • Clear ask (don't be vague)

Script 4: When They Say "We Can't Go Higher"

Don't give up – explore other options:

"I understand there may be constraints on base salary. Would you be open to discussing other aspects of the package? For example, [signing bonus / additional vacation / equity / remote work flexibility / earlier review for raise / professional development budget]?"

Why this works: Sometimes budget for base salary is truly fixed, but there's flexibility elsewhere. A $5,000 signing bonus costs the same as a $5,000 raise but comes from a different budget line.

Script 5: Handling the Counteroffer

You counter with $95,000, they come back with $88,000 (their original was $85,000). Now what?

"I appreciate you working with me on this. I was really hoping to get closer to my target of $95,000. Would $92,000 be possible?"

Or, if you're willing to close:

"I appreciate the movement to $88,000. If you can get to $90,000, I'm ready to accept and move forward."

Why this works: You're being collaborative, not adversarial. The second option signals you're ready to close, which can motivate them to find those extra dollars.

Common Objections and Responses

"This is standard for someone at your experience level."

"I understand there may be general ranges, but I bring [specific differentiators]. My research suggests that for someone with these qualifications, compensation is typically higher. Could we discuss what would be possible?"

"We don't have budget flexibility."

"I understand budget constraints. Would you be open to discussing a review in 6 months with specific goals tied to a salary adjustment? Or exploring other elements of the compensation package?"

"We need to be fair to other employees."

"I appreciate that – fairness is important. I'm asking based on my specific qualifications and market data for this role. I'm confident I'll deliver value that justifies this investment."

Negotiation Don'ts

  • Don't give ultimatums unless you mean them. "Give me $X or I walk" only works if you're actually willing to walk.
  • Don't make it personal. "I need this because of my mortgage" isn't persuasive. Focus on your value and market rates.
  • Don't negotiate via email for complex discussions. Get on a call or meet in person when possible. Tone gets lost in email.
  • Don't accept immediately. Even if the offer is great, take time to review.
  • Don't lie about other offers. It's risky and often backfires. If asked, you can say "I'm exploring several opportunities" without fabricating specific numbers.

Timing Matters

For new jobs

Negotiate after you have a written offer. Before that, you have less leverage. After you accept, you have none.

For annual reviews

Start the conversation 2-3 months before review cycles. Plant the seed early. "I'd like to discuss my compensation at my upcoming review – what would you need to see to support a significant increase?"

After a big win

Just crushed a major project? Took on significant new responsibility? Good timing to discuss compensation.

The Numbers

Here are my negotiation results:

  • Job 1: Didn't negotiate. Left $5-8K on table (I found out later).
  • Job 2: Negotiated $85K offer to $92K. +$7K.
  • Review 1: Asked for raise, got 8% instead of standard 3%. +$4K base.
  • Job 3: Negotiated $105K to $118K plus $5K signing bonus. +$18K.
  • Review 2: Negotiated promotion + 15% raise after documenting wins. +$12K base.
  • Job 4: Negotiated $125K to $135K. +$10K.

That's $51K+ in direct compensation from a few conversations. Compounded over a career with percentage-based raises, the total impact is much higher.

Practice

Negotiation is a skill. Practice helps. Some ways to prepare:

  • Role-play with a friend (one plays the hiring manager)
  • Practice saying your ask out loud (it feels different than thinking it)
  • Write out your key points beforehand
  • Anticipate objections and prepare responses

The first negotiation is the hardest. Each one after gets easier.

The Bottom Line

You will not get what you don't ask for. Companies expect negotiation. The worst outcome is hearing "no" and accepting the original offer. The best outcome is potentially thousands of dollars more, compounding over your entire career. Always negotiate.
