The FIRE Movement: A Realistic Take From Someone Pursuing It

December 30, 2025 · 14 min read

FIRE – Financial Independence, Retire Early – has been a thing on the internet for over a decade now. The basic premise sounds amazing: save aggressively, invest wisely, and retire in your 30s or 40s instead of your 60s. No more bosses, no more 9-to-5, complete freedom to do what you want with your life.

I've been pursuing FIRE for about 6 years now. Here's my honest assessment of what's real, what's exaggerated, and whether this is actually achievable for normal people.

The Basic Math of FIRE

FIRE is fundamentally about savings rate. The more you save, the faster you can retire. The math comes from the 4% rule (or 3.5% for more conservative planning): if you can live on 4% of your investments, you're financially independent.

Working backwards:

  • Need $40,000/year? You need $1,000,000 invested.
  • Need $60,000/year? You need $1,500,000 invested.
  • Need $80,000/year? You need $2,000,000 invested.

The variable that determines how fast you get there is savings rate:

Savings Rate Years to FIRE
10% 51 years
20% 37 years
30% 28 years
40% 22 years
50% 17 years
60% 12.5 years
70% 8.5 years

At 50% savings rate, starting from zero, you could retire in 17 years. Start at 23, retire at 40. Start at 30, retire at 47. That's the FIRE promise.

What the FIRE Community Gets Right

Savings rate is the key variable

The FIRE community's focus on savings rate is correct. Cutting expenses does double duty: you save more AND you need less to retire. Both factors accelerate your timeline.

Investing in index funds works

The FIRE community strongly promotes low-cost index fund investing. This isn't exotic or complicated – it's the approach that beats most active investors over the long term.

Lifestyle inflation is the enemy

FIRE folks understand that letting expenses grow with income is how people stay on the treadmill forever. Keeping lifestyle stable while income grows is the superpower.

Freedom has tremendous value

Even if you never fully "retire early," having "F-you money" (enough to walk away from a bad situation) is life-changing. The FIRE framework helps you build that.

What the FIRE Community Gets Wrong (Or Overblows)

The extreme frugality isn't sustainable for most people

Some FIRE stories involve eating rice and beans, never going out, driving ancient cars, and generally living like a monk. That works for some people but it's not realistic (or desirable) for most.

I've found a middle ground: I don't deprive myself, but I'm intentional. I spend on things I value and cut ruthlessly on things I don't. My savings rate is around 40%, not 70%. That's fine.

High income is usually required

Here's the uncomfortable truth: most FIRE success stories involve people with high incomes. Software engineers, doctors, couples with two professional salaries.

On a $50,000 income in a medium-cost-of-living area, saving 50% is nearly impossible. The math might technically work, but the execution is brutal.

FIRE is more achievable if you earn well. That's not to say lower earners can't benefit from FIRE principles – they can and should! – but the timeline expectations need adjustment.

"Retire" is the wrong word

Most FIRE people don't actually retire in the "sit on a beach doing nothing" sense. They switch to work they find meaningful, often earning some money along the way. Part-time consulting, starting businesses, creative projects.

A better framing: FIRE gives you the option to work on your terms. You might still work, but you don't HAVE to. The pressure is gone.

Healthcare is a massive wildcard (in the US)

American FIRE stories often gloss over healthcare costs. If you retire at 40, you need to cover health insurance for 25 years before Medicare. That could be $500-1,500+/month depending on family size and location.

A family spending $18,000/year on health insurance ($1,500/month) needs an extra $450,000 in investments just to cover healthcare at the 4% rule.

This is the biggest practical obstacle to early retirement in the US.

My Personal FIRE Journey

Six years ago, I discovered FIRE and got excited. I was in my late 20s, making decent money, spending all of it.

Year 1: The honeymoon phase

I cut everything. No restaurants, no subscriptions, no new clothes. Savings rate shot to 55%. I was miserable but motivated.

Year 2: The backlash

I burned out on extreme frugality. Had a few months of overspending. Felt like a failure. Savings rate dropped to 25%.

Year 3-4: Finding balance

I figured out what I actually valued. Travel and good food? Yes. New gadgets and fancy clothes? Not really. Savings rate stabilized around 40%.

Year 5-6: The long game

I've accepted this is a 15-20 year journey, not a 5-10 year sprint. I'm okay with that. I'm building wealth steadily while also living a life I enjoy today.

Current status

I'm about 35% of the way to my FIRE number. At my current pace, I'll hit it around age 50. Not the "retire at 35" dream, but still a decade earlier than traditional retirement.

Is FIRE Right for You?

FIRE makes sense if:

  • You have above-average income OR below-average expenses (ideally both)
  • You're genuinely willing to delay some gratification
  • You have a vision for what you'd do with freedom
  • You find saving and investing interesting, not torturous
  • You're in good health (health issues change the calculus)

FIRE might not be for you if:

  • Your income barely covers essentials
  • You genuinely love your career and would work regardless
  • Extreme saving would make you miserable
  • You have expensive hobbies or lifestyle needs that are non-negotiable
  • You're more interested in the "retire early" fantasy than the actual work

The FIRE Spectrum

Not everyone pursuing FIRE aims for the same thing. There are flavors:

Lean FIRE

Retiring on minimal expenses ($25,000-40,000/year). Requires ~$625,000-1,000,000. Achievable faster but means continued frugality forever.

Regular FIRE

Retiring on moderate expenses ($40,000-80,000/year). Requires ~$1,000,000-2,000,000. The mainstream FIRE target.

Fat FIRE

Retiring on higher expenses ($100,000+/year). Requires $2,500,000+. Takes longer but allows more lifestyle flexibility.

Coast FIRE

Saving aggressively early, then "coasting" – letting investments grow while only earning enough to cover current expenses. No more retirement savings needed.

Barista FIRE

Having enough invested that part-time work covers expenses. Named after the idea of working at Starbucks for health insurance.

Pick your flavor based on your preferences and constraints. There's no one right way.

Practical FIRE Advice

If you're interested in pursuing some version of FIRE:

  1. Calculate your number. Annual expenses × 25. That's your target.
  2. Calculate your current savings rate. Be honest. This determines your timeline.
  3. Find sustainable cuts. What spending doesn't actually make you happy? Cut that.
  4. Increase income if possible. The math gets much easier with higher income.
  5. Invest in boring index funds. No need to get fancy. VTI/VXUS or a target-date fund.
  6. Account for healthcare. If you're in the US, this is a real cost. Budget for it.
  7. Have a post-FIRE vision. What will you actually do with your time?
  8. Don't sacrifice your health or relationships. No point retiring early if you're alone and unhealthy.

The Bottom Line

FIRE is real and achievable for some people. It's not a scam or a fantasy. But it's also not for everyone, and the extreme early retirement stories aren't representative of most journeys.

What I think is more broadly useful: the FIRE mindset. Even if you never retire early, understanding that you can save more, that lifestyle inflation is optional, that financial freedom is possible – that's powerful.

Maybe you'll retire at 45. Maybe at 55. Maybe you'll just have enough "F-you money" to take career risks and sleep well at night. All of those outcomes are better than sleepwalking into your 60s without savings.

Take what's useful from FIRE, leave what doesn't serve you, and build the financial life that works for YOUR values and circumstances.

My Realistic FIRE Summary

I'm pursuing FIRE but on my own terms. 40% savings rate, not 70%. Retire at 50-ish, not 35. Enjoying life along the way, not white-knuckling through deprivation. FIRE is a direction, not a religion.
